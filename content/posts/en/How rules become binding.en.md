A rule can be clear on paper and disputed in practice. Who will notice a violation? Who will check whether it actually happened? And what happens if the monitor makes a mistake? The answers matter not only for compliance, but also for whether participants can challenge the rule's application.

## Who monitors the monitor?

```text
rule → observation → check the facts → decide on a response → enforcement → review
 ↑                                                                            ↓
 └──────────────────────────── report an error ────────────────────────────────┘
```

This is a map of questions, not a proven causal sequence. Who appoints the monitor, to whom are they accountable, and can an error be corrected? Can a participant safely report a problem or leave? Neither a sanction nor an election automatically resolves these questions.

## What changes when participants choose a monitor?

Delia Baldassarri and Guy Grossman conducted a *lab-in-the-field* experiment in Uganda with 1,543 farmers from 50 producer cooperatives. Participants played a public goods game. There were no sanctions in the control condition; in the other two, after two preliminary rounds, one participant was appointed as a monitor with the power to reduce players' payoffs at the monitor's own expense. The monitor was either elected by secret ballot or selected by random lottery.

Average contributions were higher in the conditions with a monitor, and higher with an elected monitor than with a randomly selected one. A difference from the control was already observed before the monitor first imposed sanctions. The contribution analysis includes 1,446 players: 97 monitors are excluded from the total. These are results from a particular game and procedure among Ugandan cooperative members, not a universal assessment of policing or organisations. [Baldassarri and Grossman, PNAS 2011](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3131358/); [copy of the paper](https://guygrossman.com/assets/pdf/2011_NAS_PE_centralized_sanctioning.pdf).

The authors explain the additional response to an elected monitor by the greater legitimacy of that monitor's authority. The experiment changed the selection procedure; behaviour alone does not allow all possible reasons for the response to be measured separately, such as expectations about what the elected person will do. Election in this game does not prove that every decision by a monitor is fair or that people have a safe appeal.

## A sanction can also support a bad rule

In another laboratory experiment, the opportunity for mutual punishment sustained participation in actions that reduced the group's total payoff. A separate second experiment studied judgments about whether refusing to participate was acceptable. This is a counterexample under the conditions of a particular game, not proof that all punishment is harmful. [Abbink and colleagues, 2017](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5607004/).

A sanction alone does not show what behaviour it reinforces or who bears the cost of an error. When assessing a system, it is necessary to distinguish the rule, the fact of a violation, the choice of response and the ability to challenge it. A reward for contributing and a punishment for refusing also create different incentives; they should not be treated as interchangeable simply because both responses affect behaviour.

## Compliance is not agreement

A person may follow a rule for different reasons: they may share its goal, expect a benefit, fear punishment or have no acceptable exit. A compliance rate alone cannot reliably tell us which reason was at work. Likewise, a high share of successful complaints may indicate either effective review or errors in initial decisions; drawing a conclusion requires data about the cases themselves.

For a practical assessment, the grounds for a response, the procedure for independent review, protection for someone reporting an error and the conditions for exit can be described in advance. These are proposals for procedural design, not results from the two games above. It is also necessary to ask whether the rule applies to the monitor and what happens when a wrongly accused participant is cleared.
