Sources differ in accessibility: the Richardson discussion uses an available excerpt; the other cases draw on the texts and archaeological data identified below. Cutamalla predates the Inca state and is not direct evidence of its institutions.

> By the end, you should understand why states do not emerge by one universal pattern, what practical problems early centres faced in Mesopotamia, China and the Andes, and how to compare a state with a large company without romanticising or demonising either.

## The main idea

When people say “the state emerged,” it is easy to picture a single ladder: village → city → king → bureaucracy → modern administration. It is convenient for a textbook but dangerous for analysis: it encourages a search for one main cause—grain, war, writing, irrigation or religion—when different regions combined factors differently.

A better question is: **what constraint became too costly for the old order, and what new intermediary made action at a distance possible?**

```text
constraint of scale
→ resources and information become visible
→ an intermediary and authority appear
→ the centre acts farther away
→ extraction and the cost of error rise
→ a new channel for feedback or resistance emerges
```

This is not a law of history. This and the following diagrams show possible relationships, not necessary sequences or measured effects. They help separate observations from explanations.

## What counts as a state?

A palace or high wall alone cannot establish that an early society was a state. Archaeologists usually look for a combination: centralised authority, an internally specialised administration, durable territorial governance and an ability to mobilise resources through intermediaries.

The boundary is blurred. A storehouse, a large temple or a military leader may precede a durable state apparatus. Conversely, rule can be uneven: a centre may control a road and tax while leaving everyday life to local communities.

Primary state formation is useful to study because it considers cases without a ready-made neighbouring state to copy. Spencer's comparative model of territorial expansion identifies several such regions, but the sample is small, the evidence archaeological and the model remains open to testing ([Spencer on primary state formation](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2867764/)).

## A historical route passport

To avoid turning comparison into a collection of vivid stories, use the same card for each case:

```text
StatePathCase = (
  initial_constraint,
  scale,
  resource_base,
  distance,
  information_carrier,
  extraction,
  integration,
  authority,
  coercion,
  bargaining,
  feedback,
  exit,
  counterexample,
  transfer_limit
)
```

An `information_carrier` need not be writing: it can be a clay tablet, household register, road, storehouse, office, ritual or network of local intermediaries. `Integration` includes exchange, patronage, resettlement, shared work, marriage ties and recognition of the centre as well as conquest. `Exit` reminds us that people without a modern right to leave might still flee, migrate, evade, rebel or move to another centre.

## How to read the three routes

Do not seek a “first cause” in the sections below. Read each case as a question about governance:

1. What constraint made action at a distance difficult?
2. What made people, resources or territory visible to the centre?
3. Who translated that knowledge into an order, exchange or obligation?
4. How did participants correct errors, bargain, resist or leave?

The sequence is useful beyond archaeology: a company may create a register, a ministry a beneficiary database, a platform a moderation system. In each case, examine the coordination benefit and the price of added power separately: who may classify, who bears an error's cost, and whether a working review channel exists.

## Route 1. Southern Mesopotamia: city, storehouse and record

### What we observe

In southern Mesopotamia's Uruk period, large cities, writing technologies and institutional political authority developed together. One historian describes this combination as material from which an early primary state is reconstructed; modern explanations dispute the roles of landscape, storage, water management and production ([Richardson, *Early Mesopotamia*](https://doi.org/10.1093/pastj/gts009)).

Early records were linked to economic accounting. A tablet dated roughly 3100–2900 BCE, probably from Uruk, records barley and, according to the museum, likely documents a temple's grain distribution ([Metropolitan Museum tablet](https://www.metmuseum.org/art/collection/search/327385)). Seals and clay accounting devices are also linked to goods in circulation ([Ferrara and colleagues](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/antiquity/article/seals-and-signs-tracing-the-origins-of-writing-in-ancient-southwest-asia/B3C2D400F3F80A7A0162D9035C9C2804)). Records made some information external and portable, but one document cannot reveal the whole administration.

### A mechanism that may have operated

```text
urban density and shared stores
→ produce can be collected and stored
→ accounting makes flows visible
→ scribes and institutions become intermediaries
→ a decision outlives a particular elder
→ the centre can direct resources beyond its personal circle
```

This is a possible explanation, not the only proven sequence. Records may have answered an existing administrative demand rather than created it. Temple, palace and city organisations may have overlapped; none should automatically be declared the sole “owner” of the early state.

### Practical benefit and cost

The benefit is **memory and comparability**: a centre can compare deliveries, plan work and pass a role to the next official. The cost is **reducing lived reality to categories**: a register of grain, rations and debt may leave a poor harvest, conflict or hidden dependence outside the decision.

```text
administrative visibility ↑
→ reach of direction ↑
→ ability to extract resources ↑
→ cost of misclassification ↑
```

## Route 2. Qin: war, registration and a central apparatus

### What we observe

China's Warring States period illustrates another route. Qin existed among competing states and grew stronger until it united its rivals in 221 BCE. A study of Qin's political ecology, drawing partly on excavated administrative documents, describes grain taxes, state granaries, labour mobilisation for projects and distant decisions about local resources. Its body also discusses household registration and infrastructure ([Lander, *Making Use of the Land*](https://doi.org/10.1017/jch.2022.19)).

Competition and war may have encouraged mobilisation, although individual mechanisms are difficult to separate from other changes. According to Lander, household registration helped the apparatus count people, taxes and labour; it did not make local ties disappear.

### A mechanism that may have operated

```text
competition between states
→ armies and supplies become more costly
→ registers, taxes and standards are needed
→ officials connect households to the centre
→ grain and labour fund roads, armies and projects
→ a more capable apparatus may gain an advantage over rivals
```

We highlight **standards and mobilisation** for comparison without ranking them against urban autonomy. The centre tried to make people and land comparable for taxation, distribution and service, but that order could be costly to those mobilised. In Lander's interpretation, heavy demands contributed to Qin's instability: this is a historical explanation, not a measurement of the sole cause of its fall. He associates early Han with lower fixed taxes and a greater role for local elites while retaining central bureaucracy.

### Practical benefit and cost

A potential benefit is **the reach of mobilisation**. A possible risk is **a vicious circle of extraction**: excessive demands may prompt evasion or resistance, followed by further coercion. This is a hypothesis to test, not an established sequence for the whole period.

```text
capacity of the apparatus ≠ regime stability
capacity of the apparatus ≠ quality of life
capacity of the apparatus ≠ legitimacy
```

## Route 3. The Andes: regional consolidation, roads and labour

### What we observe

Inca history shows that a large political order can arise in a different ecological and informational setting. Research in the Cuzco Valley using archaeological surveys and ethnohistorical materials links 1000–1400 CE to a centralised state and regional consolidation. Expansion after 1400 did not rely on one method: weaker neighbours might accept administration and patronage, strong rivals remained independent, and intermediate groups used alliances and violence ([Bauer and Covey on Inca state formation](https://doi.org/10.1525/aa.2002.104.3.846)).

That differs from a picture of an empire simply “sending orders down.” Integration varied with local balances of power. Administration had to collect resources and connect different landscapes, settlements and groups.

### Infrastructure and labour as a governance loop

Sources for the pre-colonial Andes differ from the tablet and written archives of Mesopotamia or Qin. The Inca used khipus, knotted-cord records: accounting examples have been excavated at the Inkawasi storehouse ([khipu archive study](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/latin-american-antiquity/article/accounting-in-the-kings-storehouse-the-inkawasi-khipu-archive/417CED6AB7ED0F6D37DC7A3E84F698D5)). Their interpretation remains a research subject. Historians and archaeologists also combine geographic data, archaeobotany, settlements, households and burials. Research at Cutamalla integrates terraces, settlements and dependency; its authors consider forced collective labour a probable basis of production. The site dates to approximately 250–40 BCE and predates the Inca state by more than a millennium: it is a separate regional example, not evidence of Inca institutions. The link between resource management and dependency belongs to the proposed approach and this site, not a general conclusion about the Andes ([Mader et al. on terraces and dependency](https://doi.org/10.3389/fearc.2024.1328315)).

It helps to distinguish reciprocal labour exchange, community work and compulsory labour for a wider authority. Research on contemporary Peruvian communities describes `ayni` as reciprocal assistance, `minka` as community work and `mita` as unpaid labour for public projects ([study supplement](https://static.cambridge.org/content/id/urn%3Acambridge.org%3Aid%3Aarticle%3AS0003055423000333/resource/name/S0003055423000333sup001.pdf)). Separately, research using colonial documents describes pre-colonial Inca `mit’a` as a rotational labour draft ([Huancavelica mining study](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/international-labor-and-working-class-history/article/invisible-miners-in-a-mountain-of-mercury-negotiation-health-and-night-work-in-late-16th-century-huancavelica-peru/76BF57B88148C8745FE878544EE9A9C1)). Meanings and obligations varied by place and time: modern definitions do not prove the exact design of each Inca institution.

The same outward form—people working together—can be a reciprocal norm, local coordination or coercive extraction. Calling all of it simply culture or simply slavery loses the mechanism.

### Practical benefit and cost

A benefit is **territorial connection and adaptation to different conditions**. Roads, regional centres, labour obligations and local intermediaries can support action where a uniform standard fits poorly. The cost is asymmetric dependence: a centre can turn infrastructure and a collective norm into an obligation that is hard for a particular group to escape.

## Comparing the three routes

| Field | Southern Mesopotamia | Qin | Andes / Inca |
|---|---|---|---|
| Initial constraint | urban growth, stores and memory at a distance | rival states and costly mobilisation | difficult terrain, distance and different groups |
| Main information carrier | records, units of account, institutions | household registers, standards, administrative documents | khipu, roads, settlements, labour and regional ties; archaeological traces |
| Main resource | stores and distribution of produce | tax grain and mobilised labour | land, harvests, infrastructure and collective labour |
| Integration | urban institution and accounting | registration, officials, standards and conquest | consolidation, patronage, alliances, violence and labour obligations |
| Strength | memory and comparability | speed and action at a distance | infrastructure adapted to different conditions |
| Typical failure | register hides local reality | extraction overloads the regime | shared work becomes dependence |
| Transfer limit | writing does not always create a state | war does not always create a durable apparatus | infrastructure does not prove consent |

The comparison shows different combinations of tasks, not a “winner.” A state can be strong in accounting but weak in feedback, quick to mobilise but politically fragile, or good at connecting territory while poorly limiting labour dependence.

## Four competing hypotheses

Rather than choose an explanation in advance, hold several possibilities:

```text
H1: an institution emerged because the old order could not handle scale;
H2: an elite captured the institution to extract resources;
H3: external competition pushed the centre to mobilise faster;
H4: the institution spread through borrowing, imitation or coercion.
```

They may all be true in different roles: war may create a crisis, old accounting provide infrastructure and elites direct the new apparatus towards themselves.

Test them in sequence:

1. What appeared first: threat, stores, register, office or obligation?
2. Who gained resources and who lost an earlier way to bargain?
3. Did the institution work only at the centre or also at the periphery?
4. What happened when the original threat disappeared?
5. Did appeal, bargaining and safe exit accompany control?
6. Is there a neighbouring case with a similar problem but a different institutional form?

## Not one formula, but a capacity vector

Instead of ranking societies as “primitive” or “developed,” use a vector:

```text
StateCapacity = (
  legibility, mobilisation, execution, continuity,
  adjudication, legitimacy, bargaining, feedback, exit
)
```

A rough working expression might be:

```text
EffectiveCapacity ≈
  legibility × mobilisation × execution × continuity
  − extraction_cost − error_cost − resistance_cost
```

This is a heuristic, not an equation that calculates history: units and weights are undefined, and variables may be interdependent. It prompts us to ask why a register did not improve execution, why resources did not become legitimacy, or why a central apparatus collapsed after success.

## Applying this to a modern organisation

A company, ministry or platform also chooses a way to handle a particular constraint, not the “best system in general.”

### If the problem is memory and comparability

Records, standards, roles and a shared language form the Mesopotamian analogy. Check not only whether reports exist but whether records are corrected when someone on the ground shows a category is wrong.

### If the problem is speed and mobilisation

A single mandate, resource register, chain of responsibility and fast information exchange form the Qin analogy. Check whether collecting metrics has displaced outcomes and whether dissent costs more than error.

### If conditions differ across territory

Local intermediaries, infrastructure, shared minimum rules and room to adapt execution form the Andean analogy. Local flexibility must be visible and contestable, or “adaptation” can conceal unequal coercion.

A minimum organisational card:

```text
1. What constraint defeats the old order?
2. What resource is the centre trying to see and mobilise?
3. Who holds local knowledge?
4. What intermediary translates a rule into action?
5. What does a participant receive in exchange for resources or labour?
6. How is a wrong record or classification corrected?
7. Who can contest a decision and who actually hears it?
8. How can a mandate be revoked or someone exit without destructive informal penalties?
```

## Counterexample: scale is not historical progress

Administration can improve coordination while expanding extraction. A road-building state can compel labour; a register enabling aid can also enable more precise seizure; a shared ceremony can create reciprocity or entrench inequality.

Even a successful institution can be politically unstable. Qin illustrates that capacity to mobilise grain and labour does not guarantee tolerable demands. Collective work could take different forms in different Andean contexts; present-day observations alone do not establish its Inca-era design. Separate the assessments:

```text
scale of action ≠ quality of governance
coordination ≠ consent
integration ≠ voluntariness
visibility to the apparatus ≠ justice
```

## A practical test for historical explanations

Take any account of state formation and fill in five lines:

| Question | What to look for |
|---|---|
| What task became too costly? | distance, stores, war, exchange, labour or conflict |
| What carried memory? | record, register, office, road, ritual or network |
| What new resource did the centre gain? | tax, labour, food, information, people |
| Where was the local failure? | filtered signals, resistance, error, faction |
| What could stop the centre? | bargaining, succession, migration, rebellion, appeal |

If one word, such as “war,” answers everything, the explanation is probably too coarse. A good account shows a chain, competing causes and the price of a new order.

## Conclusion

A state is not one invented institution, but a collection of ways to act beyond personal acquaintance. In Mesopotamia, city, institution and record became important intermediaries; in Qin, registration, standards, taxes, labour and competition; in the Andes, regional consolidation, infrastructure, local relationships and forms of collective work.

A general mechanism can be framed as follows: an old constraint makes distant action costly; a new information and resource loop lowers that cost; the same loop creates new power and new errors. Instead of only asking “why did the state emerge?”, ask **what it learned to see, what it could mobilise, who mediated, what participants paid and how the centre learned it was wrong**.
