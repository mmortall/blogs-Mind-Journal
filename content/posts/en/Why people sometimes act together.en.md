When many people change their behaviour at almost the same time, it is easy to imagine that one cause directs them all. But participants may see different actions by their neighbours, bear different costs and have different reasons to join. To understand an event, it helps to ask separately who received information, who believed it, who acted and whether the action persisted.

A model can show how a small signal spreads under specified rules. An experiment can test a particular change in a particular setting. Neither, by itself, explains every mass movement.

## A threshold in a network model

In Duncan Watts's model, participants occupy positions in a random network and change state according to a threshold rule based on their neighbours' actions. Under some combinations of connectivity and thresholds, a small initial shock can produce a large cascade; under others, spread is limited. [Watts, PNAS 2002, full text](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC122850/).

This is a property of the specified model, not a prediction that a particular appeal will cause a protest or that a cascade will necessarily be hard to reverse. In a real network, ties, visibility and reasons for participating may differ from the model's assumptions. The fact that many people acted does not automatically reveal what each of them believes.

## An online experiment: the researcher sets the network

Damon Centola created online health communities with artificially specified networks. The paper reports 1,528 participants recruited from health-interest websites. The six paired network-size labels in its Figure 2 add up to 1,540 network slots; the presentation does not reconcile these counts, and slots should not be assumed to be distinct people. Participants were randomly assigned to clustered-lattice or corresponding random networks. Six paired trials produced 12 separate diffusion processes, each initiated by a seed signal to one node’s neighbours. Registration spread farther and faster in clustered networks; adoption was more likely when signals came from multiple neighbours. Those signals followed neighbours’ registrations, so the number received was not separately randomized. [Centola, Science 2010, published text](https://ndg.asc.upenn.edu/wp-content/uploads/2016/04/Centola-2010-Science.pdf).

This was a particular online action in specially created networks. The result does not mean that every piece of news spreads after one contact, that every behaviour requires several signals, or that such a network reproduces the organisation of a street movement. Seeing a message, registering and participating over time are different outcomes.

## In the lab: how a rule was chosen matters

Pedro Dal Bó, Andrew Foster and Louis Putterman conducted 18 computer sessions with 276 Brown University students. Participants played a prisoner's dilemma; the researchers compared the effect of a rule selected by a vote with that of a rule imposed externally. In the author manuscript of the published article, the authors report that the rule's effect on cooperation was greater when it was chosen democratically. [Dal Bó, Foster and Putterman, author manuscript of AER 2010 article](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4112759/); [NBER working version, 2008](https://www.nber.org/papers/w13999).

This was a laboratory game with students, not a measurement of every democratic decision. The manuscript is marked as an author version; it was not compared here with the final publisher's typeset version. The experiment compares procedures under a particular rule and does not prove that elections always produce a better decision.

## In the field: an example set by a local leader

In a controlled field experiment in 52 rural Bolivian communities, participants could contribute money voluntarily towards educational materials for the local school. B. Kelsey Jack and María Recalde compared conditions in which an elected local authority made an initial public contribution, a randomly selected participant did so, or all contributions were private. In the conditions with an initial contribution, the other participants made their decisions privately after observing it. The authors found higher total contributions when the authority made the initial public contribution than when all contributions were private. Differences from the condition with a randomly selected first contributor depended on the analysis and were not statistically significant in every estimate. In six sessions assigned to the authority condition, the authority was absent and another condition was used; four later communities received unplanned authority sessions. The authors examined potential selection bias using instrumental-variable and compliant-community analyses. [Jack and Recalde, published article, 2015](https://www.povertyactionlab.org/sites/default/files/research-paper/659%20Local%20Leaders%20in%20Bolivia.pdf).

The study concerned voluntary contributions to a specific purpose in selected communities, not every form of leadership or all public goods. A visible example may convey information or change expectations; one result alone does not justify confidently choosing a single mechanism.

## In the field: who receives a programme first

In a study in 176 isolated Honduran villages, Edoardo Airoldi and Nicholas Christakis compared ways of selecting households for a 22-month health education programme. Villages were randomly assigned to conditions with different proportions of recipients and selection methods: random selection or friendship nomination. The authors assessed 117 measures of knowledge, attitudes and practices. In their results, nomination targeting at different thresholds reduced the number of households that needed to be offered the programme to reach specified levels of uptake; knowledge spread more readily than practices, and spillovers were observed up to two network steps away. [Airoldi and Christakis, author manuscript of Science 2024 article](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12903193/).

The main comparison concerns the method and proportion of initial targeting in a particular programme, not proof that every network intervention is beneficial. Results differed across measures and do not provide a universal rule for selecting people. The available version is marked as an author manuscript; it was not compared here with the final publisher's version.

## What lies beyond a cascade

The spread of a single action is not the same as a sustained movement. The latter raises questions about continued participation, resources, organisation, and opportunities to disagree or exit. One number of views or registrations cannot measure these things.

When examining a particular case, it is possible to consider separately which signals people saw, how many sources were independent, what participation cost, and whether participants could refuse. These are questions for research, not automatically established conclusions of the studies above.
