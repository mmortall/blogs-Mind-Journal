An organisation may have a complaint form, but that alone does not answer whether people know about it, will receive an understandable response, or can have an error corrected. Another consideration is the cost of exit: the option to close an account or leave a job does not by itself show that a person can use an alternative without serious loss.

## Four different questions

For this article, **voice** means reporting a problem or proposing a change to a rule; an **appeal** is a request to review a specific decision; **feedback** is the use of information about consequences to examine subsequent actions; and **exit** is the option to end participation or dependence. These are working definitions, not a complete account of any one theory.

These possibilities can exist separately. A person may be able to warn of an error but have no right to challenge a decision. A decision may be reviewed without changing the procedure that led to the error. When examining a system, it can help to follow the path: access to a channel → consideration → response → possible correction → checking for recurrence. This is a map of questions, not a proven causal sequence.

## What work teams show

Amy Edmondson defined team psychological safety as a shared belief that the team is safe for interpersonal risk-taking. In a study of 51 work teams at one manufacturing company, she found an association between psychological safety and team learning behaviour; her model also examined the association between that behaviour and team performance. Safety and learning were assessed using team-member questionnaires; performance was also rated by observers outside the teams. These observational measures do not establish that safety caused learning or performance. [Edmondson, 1999](https://web.mit.edu/curhan/www/docs/Articles/15341_Readings/Group_Performance/Edmondson%20Psychological%20safety.pdf).

This was not an experiment on introducing a complaint channel. The author explicitly notes that the cross-sectional survey design cannot demonstrate causality; one company and 51 teams limit generalisation. Responses about safety also do not prove that independent review or an accessible exit exists.

## From an individual appeal to a system-wide question

In its 2023 report, Australia's Royal Commission into the Robodebt Scheme recommended that Services Australia identify first-instance tribunal cases raising significant legal and policy issues and alert senior officers there and at the Department of Social Services. It separately recommended that the department establish, or maintain if already established, a system for identifying all significant tribunal decisions and bringing them to its secretary. The commission also recommended that the tribunal's successor devise an accessible publication system for first-instance social security decisions with significant legal conclusions or policy implications. These are recommendations, not evidence of implementation. [Commission recommendations, chapter 20](https://robodebt.royalcommission.gov.au/publications/report).

These are recommendations for a particular system, not evidence that they have already been implemented or guarantee correction. They help raise a broader question: how does an organisation learn about recurring problems from individual cases without disclosing unnecessary personal data? The official list of recommendations was checked for this passage; detailed findings about individual cases in the full report are not used here.

## What can be measured?

The following are proposals for local examination, not a validated universal scale. Before counting, define the period, the unit of observation and which cases belong in the denominator.

| Question | Possible check | What the number alone does not prove |
|---|---|---|
| Is the channel accessible? | Share of surveyed affected people who know how to report a problem | That reporting is safe or that everyone affected was surveyed |
| Is there a response? | Share of registered submissions during a period receiving a substantive response by a specified date | That the response is correct or that no submissions go unregistered |
| Can a decision be reviewed? | Review time and procedure; number and share of completed appeals resulting in a changed decision | That the remaining decisions were correct or that a high change rate is better |
| Is a recurring error corrected? | Time from confirming an error to changing a procedure, and later cases of the same kind | That the procedural change caused the number of cases to change |
| Is exit possible? | Available alternatives and the cost of losing income, data, housing or relationships | That participants who stay agree with the current arrangements |

For example, the share of changed decisions should compare changed appeals with all completed appeals in one defined group over the same period. Dividing the number of changed cases by all possible decisions and calling the result the share of successful appeals would be misleading. More importantly, filed cases are only a selected subset of possible cases: people may not know they can seek review or may lack the resources to do so.

A low number of complaints can therefore mean either few problems or barriers to reporting; a high share of upheld complaints can reflect effective review or many errors in initial decisions. Distinguishing these explanations requires information about access, the cases themselves and people who did not submit a complaint. A closed ticket does not always mean a substantive response either.

## What to examine in a procedure

The UN Guiding Principles on Business and Human Rights propose criteria for non-judicial grievance mechanisms: legitimacy, accessibility, predictability, equitability, transparency, rights compatibility and continuous learning. For operational-level mechanisms, they additionally specify engagement and dialogue with stakeholders. The commentary to principle 29 says operational-level mechanisms should not block access to judicial or other non-judicial mechanisms or undermine legitimate trade unions in labour disputes. This is normative guidance for the relevant mechanisms, not the result of an experiment or an assessment of every complaint channel. [UN Guiding Principles, principles 29 and 31](https://www.ohchr.org/Documents/Publications/GuidingPrinciplesBusinessHR_EN.pdf).

A local examination can separately ask who reviews a contested decision, whether an explanation and advice are available, how long the procedure takes, and what happens after a recurring error is identified. These questions do not replace legal review of the applicable rules. When publishing results, organisations should consider personal data protection and the risk of identifying a complainant, especially in small groups.

Measures are useful when they help show where the path from a report to review breaks down. They do not turn silence into agreement or, by themselves, show whether someone can leave without an unacceptable cost.
