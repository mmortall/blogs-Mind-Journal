> The aim of this article is a working map of compliance: to separate resource and sanction from office, expertise and recognised right, and then to check where voice, appeal and exit sit.

The word "management" often hides the main question: **why do people do what is asked of them at all?** One answer: they were forced. Another: they consider the demand right or lawful. A third: the manager has knowledge, resources, reputation or access to the right connections.

These mechanisms can lead to the same outward result: a person carried out an order, an employee accepted a rule, a citizen paid a tax, a community member attended a ritual. But the durability and the cost of such a result differ. Fear can produce a quick effect, but it can hide bad news. Authority lowers the cost of constant control, but it can turn into unverifiable loyalty. Expertise helps with a complex task, but it creates dependence on whoever controls the knowledge.

## One order, different reasons

Imagine that a shift supervisor asks to stop a batch after a strange signal in the control system. A worker may stop for different reasons:

| Reason | What actually works |
|---|---|
| The supervisor controls bonuses or access to shifts | power of resources |
| The post carries the right to make such a decision | authority of office |
| The rule is explained in advance, applied the same way, and open to checking | legitimacy of procedure |
| A question or refusal will be punished | fear and dependence |
| "This is how things are done here" | habit and norm |

From outside, the result is the same: the batch is stopped. But for management these are different systems. Fear can hide the next signal; an office can work only with a clear mandate; a legitimate procedure can outlive a change of supervisor; a habit can continue after the purpose of the rule has disappeared. So the fact of compliance alone does not tell us why the system holds or how it corrects its errors.

## First, separate three concepts

A convenient working scheme. Here `Power` is the capacity to change behaviour or access to a resource, `Authority` is a recognised right to demand, and `Legitimacy` is the belief that this right and procedure deserve compliance:

```text
Power = capacity to change behaviour, opinion or access to a resource
Authority = Power + recognised right to demand
Legitimacy = belief that this right and procedure deserve compliance
```

This is an analytical model, not the only definitions used in all schools. What matters is this: the resource of power, the right to demand, and the recognition of that right can diverge.

A manager may hold a formal post but lack real knowledge and respect. A charismatic leader may hold no official post yet receive obedience. A state may possess police and still lose legitimacy. A closed group may speak of voluntariness yet hold a member through housing, income and the severing of outside ties.

So instead of asking "is there power or not?", it is more useful to fill in a vector:

```text
Authority = (
  resources,
  sanctions,
  information,
  office,
  expertise,
  identity,
  recognition,
  appeal,
  cost of refusal
)
```

The diagram below shows why formal authority and real control are different things. The text version next to it serves small screens, reading without images, and screen readers.

```text
resource / sanction / expertise
              ↓
       formal authority
              ↓
        real control
          ↙           ↘
      voice          exit
          ↘           ↙
          correction or change
```

What to check here: authority is durable if a participant can report an error, get it reviewed, and leave without destructive informal punishment.

*Alternative text:* resources, sanctions and expertise create formal authority and real control; voice and exit form two channels of correction or change.

## Six sources of social influence

The French and Raven model (as presented in Raven, 1992) identifies the basic sources of power: reward, coercion, legitimate position, expertise, identification with a leader, and informational influence ([The Bases of Power](https://files.eric.ed.gov/fulltext/ED351648.pdf)). It is useful to understand these not as types of people but as channels through which one participant can change another's behaviour.

| Source | What it controls | Why a person follows | Main risk |
|---|---|---|---|
| Reward | money, status, access, help | expected gain | dependence on a single distributor |
| Coercion | fine, dismissal, exclusion, physical harm | expected loss | hiding problems and retaliatory hostility |
| Legitimate position | the recognised right of an office or procedure | "he is entitled to demand this" | office mistaken for competence |
| Expertise | rare knowledge and the ability to solve a problem | "he knows better" | expert monopoly and unverifiability |
| Identification | the image of "one of us", example, personal closeness | wish to be like or belong | personality cult and punishment for distance |
| Information | access to facts, interpretations and channels of communication | change of picture of the world | monopoly on explanation and manipulation |

In real systems the sources mix. A doctor has expertise, but the hospital gives them the post. A manager distributes bonuses, and corporate culture makes their demands habitual. A preacher offers meaning, and the community decides who gets housing and social support.

One should ask not only "what type of power is this?" but also "which sources can be checked independently, and what happens if the participant does not agree?"

## Weber: why people consider submission right

Max Weber proposed three ideal types of legitimate domination: traditional, charismatic and legal-rational ([Max Weber, Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/weber/)). These are not a ready classification of countries, nor successive stages through which every society must pass. They are three different answers to the question "why does an order have the right to be an order?"

### Traditional

"It has always been so." The right to demand is linked to a lineage, custom, age, ritual or an inherited role. The strength of this order is predictability and clear continuity. Its weakness is difficulty of revision: a bad rule is protected by the mere fact of its age.

### Charismatic

"This person can be followed because he is unusual and leads to a new goal." Such a source helps mobilise people in a crisis or when a new movement is founded. But it depends on the personal leader. After his error, death or loss of special status the question arises: who inherits the right to demand, and by what procedure?

### Legal-rational

"The office and the rule exist independently of any particular person." Bureaucracy, regulation and procedure allow one official to be replaced by another. This reduces dependence on personality, but it creates a new danger: people begin to obey the form even when the rule has stopped solving its original task.

Historically these forms do not fully replace one another. A modern organisation may have formal offices, a founder's tradition and a charismatic brand. A state may use law, national ritual and the image of a saviour. A religious community may combine ancient custom, the personal authority of a mentor and a written charter.

## Legitimacy is more than consent

A person may carry out an order without considering it right. That is why obedience and legitimacy do not coincide. Legitimacy is a relation between an authority and those it influences: the history of interaction, the fairness of procedure, respect and the possibility of being heard ([Tyler, 1997: The psychology of legitimacy](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/15661667/)).

It is useful to distinguish four levels:

```text
compliance → acceptance of procedure → trust in the authority → willingness to help and to report bad news
```

At the first level, sanction does the work. At the second, a person considers the procedure admissible even when they lose. At the third, they expect the authority to apply the rule sensibly. At the fourth, the participant supports the system and helps it learn.

All four levels cannot be measured with one question, "do you agree with the decision?" A person may dislike the outcome but consider the process fair. Or publicly support the outcome while staying silent out of fear.

## Legitimacy can be checked by behaviour, but cannot be reduced to it

In a public-goods experiment, the researchers separated an authority's attempt to make enforcement fairer from the material result of that step. In the main experiment with university students, the attempt raised the probability of contributing to the public good by about 10–12 percentage points through an intrinsic channel, holding material incentives fixed; a separate experiment showed that the effect was not reduced to a simple wish to "pay back" the authority. This is stronger than an ordinary correlation, but it is still the result of a controlled game, not proof that any honest manager will automatically earn trust ([Dickson, Gordon and Huber](https://doi.org/10.1126/sciadv.abj7377)).

It is practically useful to separate four layers:

```text
LegitimacyComplianceCase = (
  authority_source, rule_content, process_fairness, consistency,
  participation, accuracy, voice, sanction_severity, trust,
  intrinsic_compliance, extrinsic_compliance, correction, appeal,
  exit, scope_condition
)
```

A sanction does not act in a vacuum either. Tax-compliance research links procedural fairness with voluntary compliance; in one of these studies the effect of procedural fairness is stronger when trust in authorities is low and weaker when it is high (an experiment and a field study; abstract). Another field-and-experiment study finds that more severe sanctions work better when the procedure is perceived as fair, through the assessment of the authority as a moral agent. This is not a recommendation to strengthen punishment: the question is whether compliance holds when the sanction can be appealed, checked and lifted ([van Dijke](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.joep.2009.10.005), [Verboon and van Dijke](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.joep.2010.09.007)).

For the state and the firm there is an important counterweight. A review of procedural justice finds a durable link between perceived fairness, legitimacy and compliance, but the direct causal effect of changing an actual procedure remains insufficiently tested. In organisational meta-analysis, justice is linked to trust, commitment, performance and citizenship: the links to performance and citizenship are partly mediated by the quality of exchange (trust, organisational commitment, organisational support, LMX) and by affect; for counterproductive behaviour such mediation was not apparent in the model. The review covered 493 independent samples; many primary studies measured justice and exchange indicators by self-report at the same time, which can inflate associations. Modelled mediation alone does not establish a causal pathway. So "people like the procedure" is still not the same as good service, an accurate decision or a safe system for a minority ([Nagin and Telep](https://doi.org/10.1146/annurev-lawsocsci-110316-113310), [Colquitt et al.](https://doi.org/10.1037/a0031757)).

A minimal practical test: keep content and the resource outcome constant, and change only the possibility of voice, explanation, accuracy, consistency, independent appeal and access to exit. Count voluntary and sanctioned compliance separately, along with correction latency, bad news, repeated participation and harm to those who lost. If the rule disappears once pressure is removed, the result cannot be called legitimacy alone. If public agreement grows while voice, appeal and independent signals shrink, this may be an order of fear.

## Why fear gives order and at the same time breaks management

Coercion is not useless. Any system may need to stop violence, protect a boundary, or sanction a destructive act. The problem begins when fear becomes the main channel of management.

Fear changes the flow of information:

```text
error → fear of punishment → concealment → the manager sees success → the error grows
```

If a participant knows that bad news is more dangerous than the problem itself, they optimise not the result but the report. This is familiar to a state, a firm and a religious community alike. A system may look disciplined precisely at the moment when feedback has already disappeared.

Research on obedience to authority shows that behaviour depends on the experimenter's directiveness, legitimacy and consistency; on the immediacy and closeness of contact with the learner; on the distance from the experimenter; and on group pressure to disobey (a meta-analysis of 21 conditions, N = 740, with eight such factors; the authors reconstructed the data from condition sizes and counts reaching 450 V, coding condition features including teacher gender and learner vulnerability; these two codes were not among the eight significant predictors in the multivariable model, which does not show that individual traits have no effect). Popular retellings of Milgram's experiments often simplify this picture ([Meta-Milgram](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3976349/), [review of obedience](https://www.annualreviews.org/content/journals/10.1146/annurev-lawsocsci-110316-113710)).

The practical conclusion is not that "people blindly obey". The conclusion is that the institutional environment changes the cost of refusal and the availability of an alternative signal. Public disagreement from colleagues may not be a failure of loyalty but a safeguard against a mistaken order.

## Expert authority: help and dependence

A complex system cannot teach every participant from scratch each time. So it delegates the decision to a specialist. This saves time but creates an asymmetry: the expert knows more about the task than the person who must control them.

Good delegation answers four questions:

1. which exact question has been handed to the expert;
2. which data and methods they must show;
3. who checks their result independently;
4. when the mandate expires or is withdrawn.

A bad system says: "he is a specialist, so you cannot argue." A good one says: "he has a mandate in area X until date Y; here are the quality criteria; here is the channel for disagreement; here is the way to replace the expert."

Expertise turns into hidden power when the specialist at the same time chooses the metric, controls the data, evaluates themselves and decides who has the right to criticise them. In that case the problem is no longer that they may be wrong, but that the error has no independent route of detection.

## Power in the state, the firm and the closed group

One and the same map helps compare different institutions.

| System | Main source of power | What makes order durable | What can hide the problem |
|---|---|---|---|
| State | office, coercion, law, taxes, infrastructure | a common standard and distant enforcement | the centre sees convenient reports, not local reality |
| Firm | contract, salary, office, expertise, key performance indicator (KPI) | fast exchange of resources and clear responsibility | the employee adjusts the metric and stays silent about side effects |
| Religious community | meaning, ritual, identity, leader, mutual help | a shared picture of the world and strong ties | criticism is declared a spiritual defect |
| Closed group | a combination of meaning, resources, isolation and sanctions | high mutual dependence | exit becomes expensive and external checking impossible |
| Peer network | reputation, verification, access to infrastructure, community norms | visible record of contribution and voluntary participation | an informal elite controls the rules and the settlement of disputes |

The word "legitimacy" should not justify any order. A system may be legitimate for some participants and harmful to others. That is why independent rights to disagree are needed, especially when a decision affects people who did not take part in making it.

## A practical passport of power

To analyse an organisation, fill in the card not from declarations but from observed behaviour:

```text
PowerCase = (
  who demands,
  what exactly is demanded,
  which source of power is used,
  who controls information,
  who distributes the resource,
  who applies the sanction,
  who checks the person applying the sanction,
  what the dissenter receives,
  how to file an appeal,
  how to change the delegate,
  how to exit,
  which signal will show the error
)
```

The check takes less time if you look for six observable events:

1. What happens after a calm question to the leader?
2. Can you see primary data, not only an interpretation?
3. Is the rule applied the same way to the manager and to the subordinate?
4. Can a dissenter keep their status, income and outside ties?
5. Is there a body that does not answer to the person being complained about?
6. Can the decision be reversed before the damage becomes irreversible?

If the answer to all these questions is no, a formal post or an attractive ideology does not create accountable authority. It creates a concentration of power.

## The historical evolution of sources of power

History can be read as a series of ways of coordinating that became cheaper:

```text
custom → personal authority → record and office → bureaucracy → organisation and contract → algorithmic ranking
```

Custom saves explanation. A personal leader saves negotiation at a turning point. A record saves memory. An office separates the decision from the person. A contract sets the boundary of an organisation. Algorithmic ranking speeds up the comparison of many participants.

Each layer at once solves a problem and creates a new source of dependence. A record makes an action checkable, but it gives the keeper of the archive power over visibility. An office makes power replaceable, but it allows people to obey a rule even when the result is bad. Ranking speeds up feedback, but it turns complex quality into a single indicator.

So the evolution of management is not a move from "bad" power to "good" power. It is a shift of the centre of gravity: from memory to record, from person to office, from office to metric, and from metric to a system that can recommend or punish automatically.

## What this means for Sytrism

For Sytrism the question is not "who will be in charge: a human or an AI?" The more important question is how to separate the sources of power and not let one centre own all of them at once.

A minimal architecture:

* knowledge is distributed among independent sources;
* an expert receives a narrow, limited and revocable mandate;
* the procedure separates fact from the choice of values;
* the executor does not appoint their own assessor;
* dissent is recorded and protected from punishment;
* a leader, an expert or an AI cannot cancel an appeal by a single order;
* a participant can change delegate or leave without destroying their basic life.

The main test of authority is simple: **can a person say "no" without disappearing from the system, and can the system learn that they were right?** If not, we are looking not at sustainable management but at dependence disguised as consent.

## Counterexample: carrying out an order does not prove authority

A worker may carry out an order because of a threat, loss of income, or the absence of alternatives. Outwardly this looks like discipline, but once the sanction disappears the system may stop working, and bad news never reaches the top. To tell the mechanisms apart, one needs observations of the cost of refusal, access to appeal, the replaceability of the leader, and the ability to keep basic ties after disagreement.
