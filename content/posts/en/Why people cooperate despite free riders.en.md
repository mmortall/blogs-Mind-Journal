Suppose residents want to maintain a shared canal. Everyone benefits if water reaches the fields, but repairs take time. A person might hope to get water while leaving the work to others. This is one problem of collective action: a shared benefit does not by itself tell us who will contribute, or on what terms.

## What to examine in a shared problem

```text
need → contribution → observation of the result → discussion → change to the rule
 ↑                                                                  ↓
 └──────────────────────── feedback ─────────────────────────────────┘
```

This is a map of questions, not a proven sequence of causes. Who benefits and who pays? Can contributions be seen without excessive surveillance? Who can report an error, challenge a decision or leave? The answers depend on how the problem is organised and how power is distributed.

In a strict model, a public good is difficult to provide to some people while excluding others; one person's use generally does not reduce its availability to others. A common-pool resource such as water is different: exclusion can also be difficult, but one person's use reduces what remains available to others. In practice these properties can be matters of degree, not labels for every joint project.

## An experiment: paying to punish

In a laboratory experiment by Ernst Fehr and Simon Gächter, 112 students took part in a public goods game in groups of four. In some conditions, after deciding how much to contribute, participants could punish others at their own expense; in others, they could not. With both fixed groups and repeated random reassignment of participants, average contributions were higher when punishment was possible. The authors also observed that a contribution lower than the others' was more often accompanied by stronger punishment.

In both fixed and randomly reassigned groups, average payoffs with punishment were initially lower than without it but became higher toward the end of the experiment. The conditions also differed in what information about contributions participants received. This experiment had specific payoffs, rules and a limited number of rounds; it does not prove that punishment always improves cooperation or welfare. The account here uses the open [Fehr and Gächter working paper, 1999](https://www.econ.uzh.ch/apps/workingpapers/wp/iewwp010.pdf); the [AER journal publication, 2000](https://doi.org/10.1257/aer.90.4.980) is listed separately. The working and journal versions have not been compared page by page.

## A shared resource: distribution as well as contribution

Elinor Ostrom and Roy Gardner examined irrigation systems in Nepal, where farmers at the head and tail of a canal face different conditions. Their work combines a bargaining model with data on irrigation systems; it shows that users can create their own rules for allocating water and maintaining the system. The comparison of systems described was not a randomised experiment and does not by itself prove that self-governance is always better than external management. [Ostrom and Gardner, 1993](https://pubs.aeaweb.org/doi/pdfplus/10.1257/jep.7.4.93).

Punishment does not necessarily protect a public good. In another laboratory experiment, the opportunity for mutual punishment sustained contributions under two payoff rules: in one, contributions reduced the group’s total payoff; in the other, they produced no group gain before punishment costs. A second experiment examined judgments about the acceptability of not contributing under the first rule. This result concerns a particular game and shows a risk, not that punishment is universally harmful. [Abbink and colleagues, 2017](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5607004/).

## Culture and research conditions

A meta-analysis by Spadaro and colleagues covered 1,506 studies, 2,271 estimates and 183,697 participants across 70 societies. The authors considered impersonal cooperation among strangers in the Prisoner's Dilemma and public goods games. Accounting for study characteristics, they found little variation between societies and did not find that classifying societies into cultural groups explained it. The samples were not representative of their societies: 85% of studies used students, and 42% were conducted in the United States. Coverage was also uneven: some societies had very few observations, limiting how far the findings can be generalized. This is a finding about the experimental tasks studied, not proof that cultural differences are absent from all forms of cooperation. [Spadaro and colleagues, 2022](https://research.vu.nl/ws/portalfiles/portal/248159432/Cross_Cultural_Variation_in_Cooperation.pdf).

## What to do about the risk of free riding

First, it helps to ask which particular action a rule rewards or punishes. The risk of a sanction for not contributing and the risk of mistakenly punishing a participant affect a decision differently; not every risk of punishment can be written as the same cost of contributing. A person's share of the benefit, the cost of participation, information and alternatives also matter. These are guides for discussion, not a validated formula for behaviour.

It is then possible to check whether rules are clear, whether a mistake can be corrected and a sanction appealed, who can see how benefits are distributed, and what leaving would cost. These are proposals for procedural design, not conclusions established by the experiments cited. Silence or outward compliance with a rule does not by itself show that participants agree.
