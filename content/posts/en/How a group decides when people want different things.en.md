A council is discussing a new bus route. One person questions the cost estimate, another values convenience more than speed, and a third asks who will pay for the timetable change. Before choosing, it helps to distinguish three questions: what is known, which consequences participants prefer, and who can carry out the decision.

## From the question to a review

```text
facts and uncertainty → preferences → choice rule → authority → action → review
       ↑                                                          ↓
       └──────────────── feedback and reconsideration ─────────────┘
```

This is a map of questions, not a proven causal law. Which sources do participants use? Who will be affected by the choice? Who receives authority and resources? How can someone report an error or challenge it? Review and the right to reconsider need separate arrangements: they do not arise automatically after a vote.

## Estimating facts and exchanging views

When a question has a verifiable numerical answer, the group can first collect separate estimates, then show participants other information and compare the result. A separate answer does not guarantee independent errors: people may rely on the same incorrect source.

In a study by Joshua Becker, Devon Brackbill and Damon Centola, 1,360 people estimated numerical quantities online. Participants were assigned to two network conditions or a control; networks contained 40 people each. Each person first gave an estimate of their own, then in two rounds saw the average estimate of directly connected participants and could revise their answer. Control participants revised their answers without that information.

In the decentralised networks studied, the mean and median of group estimates moved closer to the correct answer on average, even as the spread of opinions narrowed. In centralised networks, the change depended on the central participant's initial estimate: it could move the group either toward or away from the answer. This is a result from a particular experiment involving estimation tasks and monetary rewards for accuracy; it does not prove that every decentralisation improves decisions or that discussions of values should be organised in the same way. [Becker, Brackbill and Centola, PNAS 2017](https://ndg.asc.upenn.edu/wp-content/uploads/2017/06/PNAS-2017-1615978114-Collective-Intelligence.pdf).

In another laboratory study, 144 students made five successive estimates of factual quantities. Two conditions provided the average or individual estimates of others; a control provided no such feedback. Exposure to others' estimates substantially narrowed diversity while collective error changed only slightly. The authors note a small decline in collective error, especially with average feedback, partly supported by additional significance tests. Together, these studies show that exchanges of estimates cannot be assumed automatically helpful or harmful; their procedures differed. [Lorenz and colleagues, PNAS 2011](https://www.sg.ethz.ch/publications/2011/lorenz2011how-social-influence/PNAS-2011-Lorenz-9020-5.pdf).

## Preferences can form a cycle

Even if each person ranks the options consistently, the result of pairwise votes can be cyclic. Suppose three equally sized groups have these preferences:

```text
A: 1 > 2 > 3
B: 2 > 3 > 1
C: 3 > 1 > 2
```

Two groups prefer 1 to 2, two prefer 2 to 3, and two prefer 3 to 1. This is an arithmetic example, not an observation of a real vote. It does not make a choice impossible, but it shows why the order of comparisons and the rule for resolving a cycle should be stated in advance.

## What a simple majority model proves

Consider a different, factual question with two answers, one of which is correct. Suppose three participants independently give the correct answer with the same probability, 0.6. In this model, the probability of a correct majority decision is `3 × 0.6² × 0.4 + 0.6³ = 0.648`. This follows from the stated assumptions; it is not an estimate of the accuracy of a particular council. A common source of error, differing competence or dependent answers can change the result; a model with one correct answer may not fit a question about values.

## From choice to action

After a decision, it helps to record who will carry it out, what resources the implementer has, and when the result will be reviewed. It is also useful to preserve the argument for dissent, specify a procedure for appeal and set conditions for reconsideration. These are proposals for procedural design, not consequences established by the two experiments above. A vote can complete a choice, but does not by itself provide a budget or eliminate harm to a minority.
