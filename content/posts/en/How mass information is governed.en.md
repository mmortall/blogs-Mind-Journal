The information environment affects which questions become visible and which explanations people may encounter. Editorial decisions, connections between people, platform rules and readers' own choices all shape it. To understand the process, it helps to distinguish publishing a message, spreading it, displaying it on a screen, clicking a link and changing a belief. These are different events: the number of shares does not show how many people believed a message.

## A false message and its spread

Soroush Vosoughi, Deb Roy and Sinan Aral studied the spread of fact-checked claims on Twitter from 2006 to 2017. The study covered approximately 126,000 rumor cascades, spread by about three million users more than 4.5 million times. The authors identified cascades using English-language replies linking to six fact-checking organizations and traced the original posts and their retweets. One rumor could produce several independent cascades. A cascade starts with a separate post and may have no retweets. [Vosoughi, Roy and Aral, Science 2018, full published text hosted by MIT](https://politics.media.mit.edu/papers/Vosoughi_Science.pdf).

In this sample, false claims spread farther and faster than true ones; the differences were especially marked for political claims. But the sample concerned claims that came to the attention of fact-checking organizations, not all messages on the network. The authors also discuss possible selection bias and separately examined another sample. Observing retweets does not measure readers' beliefs, authors' intentions or the consequences of each message. The result does not establish exactly why a person shares a particular message and does not describe every platform.

## What friends share and what appears in the feed

Eytan Bakshy, Solomon Messing and Lada Adamic examined de-identified data from 10.1 million active US Facebook users who had voluntarily stated their political affiliation in their profiles. The data covered July 2014 to January 2015. The researchers compared links shared by friends, their appearance in the ranked feed and users' clicks on links. For their analysis of news and public-affairs content, they restricted the set to 226,000 links shared by at least 20 users who had stated their political affiliation. [Bakshy, Messing and Adamic, Science 2015, published article in a university copy](https://redirect.cs.umbc.edu/courses/graduate/691/spring21/pdf/1130.full.pdf).

The authors found that the ranked feed contained somewhat less content that crossed a user's political outlook than the potential set of links from friends; choices to click were also associated with a smaller share of such content. The article compares different stages of selection and different measures. Figures for ranking and clicking cannot be added together or presented as the proportion of people who changed their minds.

This was an observational study of a particular group of users and a particular platform during a particular period. People who volunteered their political affiliation do not necessarily represent all users; link classification also depends on the method used. Overlapping factors affect ranking and choice, so the study does not establish a simple independent contribution of each mechanism for everyone, or measure what people believed after clicking.

## Reach is not persuasion

The studies answer different questions. The first measures cascades of fact-checked claims and retweets; the second examines potentially available links, feed display and clicks in a selected group. None of these measures alone captures a change in beliefs. Establishing an effect on beliefs or actions requires separate measurements and a suitable research design.

When discussing the governance of information, it is therefore useful to ask who sets access and ranking rules, which sources are visible, how a claim can be checked and how an erroneous decision can be challenged. These are questions for assessing a particular system, not a finding that one way of governing information is always more effective or fairer than another.

> Editorial limitation: the results concern the specified Twitter and Facebook data, periods and selection methods. Display, clicking and sharing are not equated with persuasion. The claim about the strength of effects from the Amsalem and Zoizner meta-analysis is not used here: its full primary text was not examined for this preparation. No author or language review has been performed.
