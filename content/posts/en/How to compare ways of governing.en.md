When one way of governing is declared more successful than another, it is tempting to find a common measure and draw up a ranking. But even similar words — “oversight”, “losses”, “accountability” — can mean different things. A more useful starting point is to ask: what exactly changed, what was the result compared with, and how was it measured?

Two field experiments help us explore these questions. One concerns road construction in Indonesian villages, the other social payments in the Indian state of Andhra Pradesh. They do not compare countries with each other or test the same reform. Comparing them here illustrates a method; it is not a ranking of systems of governance.

## Roads: what an audit can reveal

Benjamin Olken studied more than 600 village road projects in Indonesia. In randomly selected projects, a government audit was announced in advance, raising the probability of an audit from a baseline of 4% to 100%. In the published study, the discrepancy between reported expenditure and an independent engineering estimate of costs was about eight percentage points lower under this intervention. This is an estimate of an expenditure discrepancy, not an amount of theft established in court: the engineering calculation itself depends on assumptions. [Olken, 2007](https://economics.mit.edu/sites/default/files/publications/Monitoring%20Corruption%20Evidence%20from%20a%20Field.pdf).

Other measures in the experiment sought to involve residents in oversight. Their average effect was small; the author found a reduction in the measured discrepancy only where both free-rider problems and capture by local elites were limited. This does not mean that residents’ participation is always useless or that audits eliminate every kind of abuse. The study also found signs of a possible shift to other forms of behaviour, although their interpretation is ambiguous.

## Payments: technology inside an organisation

Karthik Muralidharan, Paul Niehaus and Sandip Sukhtankar studied the phased introduction of biometric smartcards for payments under the NREGS employment programme and the SSP pension programme. The order of rollout was randomly assigned across 157 subdistricts with a combined population of around 19 million people. That is the population of the areas, not the number surveyed or the number of payment recipients. [Muralidharan, Niehaus and Sukhtankar, 2016](https://poverty-action.org/sites/default/files/publications/Muralidharan_et_al_2016a.pdf).

The authors compared areas with different rollout schedules, rather than only people who had already received a card. Implementation remained incomplete: after about two years, around half of payments across both programmes in rollout areas went through the new system. For NREGS, the study found faster and more predictable payments and lower measured leakage without an average deterioration in access to the programme. The state did not require biometric authentication for payment. The authors suggest this may have helped prevent exclusion when people could not obtain cards or authenticate; the study does not isolate that policy as the cause of the access result. These findings concern the implementation and measures studied; they do not prove that every biometric system protects every recipient or prevents all abuse.

## Compare questions, not add up results

In the first case, the intervention changed the probability that projects would be audited, and the outcome was a measured expenditure discrepancy. In the second, the order in which payment infrastructure was introduced changed, and researchers measured, among other things, receipt of payments, time and leakage. The percentage points from the first study cannot be directly compared with the measures in the second: the programmes, units of observation and methods of measurement differ.

These cases suggest a set of questions for assessing other decisions: who makes the decision, what information is available to implementers and recipients, who checks discrepancies, how are errors corrected, and who might be excluded? This is a framework for further inquiry, not a finding that the experiments measured each of these points. Even a randomised result in one place does not guarantee the same effect under different rules, resources and ways of implementing them.

## Sources and limits

Both sources are published full-text articles; each describes its own field experiment and measures. The comparison between them here is analytical and is not a separate experiment. The results do not establish the general superiority of audits, participation or biometrics, and they do not replace studying consequences for groups that average estimates may conceal.
