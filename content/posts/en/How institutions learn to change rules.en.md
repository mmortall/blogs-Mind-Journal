After a decision goes wrong, an organisation may correct an individual case, change an instruction or reconsider the goal itself. But a change on paper does not yet show that the new practice works better. To assess that, we need to know what happened to people and outcomes after the change, and retain a record of why the decision was made.

## Two questions about an error

Suppose applications are processed slowly. A shortage of staff may be the reason; in that case, resources and implementation deserve examination. But the approval rule itself may also cause delay; then we need to examine what each stage is for and what removing it would cost. This is an example for reflection, not an established diagnosis of a particular organisation.

Correcting implementation and reconsidering a rule call for different questions. Did the signal reach those authorised to change the process? What exactly changed? Did new errors appear? Who bore the costs? This sequence helps organise an examination, but does not prove that one change caused another.

## Use what is known or search for something new

James March distinguished the search for new possibilities from refining known ways of working. In his 1991 paper he considered models of mutual learning between individuals and an organisational code, as well as competition for advantage. In these models, rapidly reinforcing a known approach can bring a short-term benefit and constrain later search; search also consumes resources and does not guarantee success. In one simulation, people and an organisational code adjust their beliefs to one another; a mix of faster and slower adjustment produced greater equilibrium knowledge than a uniform rate with the same average. These are modelled adjustment rates, not measurements of employees’ abilities. [March, 1991, published text](https://hiveresearchlab.org/wp-content/uploads/2013/12/march_1991_exploration_and_exploitation_in_organizational_learning.pdf).

These are conclusions and arguments of a model under its assumptions, not a measured effect of a universal ratio between exploration and current work. The paper does not tell a particular organisation how much time or money to allocate. It is also important to distinguish benefits to an organisation from consequences for the people on whom a change is tested.

## A pilot and evidence are different things

A trial rollout can help adjust implementation, test an alternative or present a chosen direction to other participants. These purposes can coexist. To understand what was learned, it is useful to record the question, comparison, measure, review period and decision rule in advance. The absence of random assignment does not make every pilot useless, but the method of comparison affects what conclusions about causes can be drawn.

Stefanie Ettelt, Nicholas Mays and Pauline Allen analysed three health and social care pilots in England from the mid- to late 2000s. In the available author manuscript, they describe how randomised evaluations were expected to resolve remaining uncertainty about the chosen direction and were also used as instruments of persuasion. This is an analysis of particular cases, not a claim that every pilot is designed as propaganda. [Ettelt, Mays and Allen, author manuscript](https://scispace.com/pdf/policy-experiments-investigating-effectiveness-or-confirming-2j6fo87cck.pdf); [publisher record](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/1356389015590737).

The copy examined identifies publication in *Evaluation* in 2015, but its text was not compared here with the final journal version. The authors study the use of evaluations in the policy process; this analysis does not yield one numerical estimate of the benefit of all pilots.

## Success depends on the question

Kath Checkland and colleagues studied the Vanguard programme in England's National Health Service. They consider pilot success in terms of process, outcomes and longer-term effects, separately considering programmatic and political dimensions. Their analysis draws on documents, interviews and local case studies; the paper was published online in 2021 and in a journal issue in 2023. The authors studied six local sites in depth and assessed success from the perspective of the pilot initiator, NHS England, while interviewing participants with varied roles. [Checkland and colleagues, open published text](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/padm.12790).

In their analysis, assessments of the programme were mixed. For example, the authors discuss a policy document's use of one positive indicator where the source of the graph was insufficiently described. This is a study of a particular programme and the authors' interpretation, not proof that every positive indicator is unreliable or that all claimed effects are caused by political motives.

## How to retain a lesson

For a project of one's own, it is possible to define in advance who receives information about errors, where initial expectations are recorded, who has authority to reconsider a decision, and what happens to participants if it is stopped. Actual outcomes can then be compared with the initial question and unexpected consequences recorded separately. These are proposals for examination, not a set of guarantees tested in the cited studies.

A report, a changed instruction and a lasting improvement are different observations. Even if an indicator rises after a change, without a suitable comparison we cannot confidently attribute the entire shift to the new procedure. A retained lesson is useful for more than scaling up: it may show that an earlier decision should be limited, reconsidered or stopped.
