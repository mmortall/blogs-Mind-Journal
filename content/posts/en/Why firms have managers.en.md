An organisation chart makes it easy to find the person who signs off on a decision. It is harder to see who first notices a problem, or whether the message reaches someone with the authority to fix it. A manager has formal authority; a worker may have knowledge without which an instruction could turn out badly.

## A map of a decision

```text
worker's signal → manager's consideration → decision → action
       ↑                                                ↓
       └──────── report on the outcome ← outcome ───────┘
```

This diagram is a set of questions, not a proven causal chain. Who sees an error? Who must consider the message? Can the worker find out why a suggestion was rejected? What changes if the person reporting a problem fears the consequences?

## Why a contract cannot solve everything

A firm can coordinate related tasks within an organisation instead of making a separate transaction every time. Internal organisation has costs too. In [“The Nature of the Firm”](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1468-0335.1937.tb00002.x) (1937), Ronald Coase compared the cost of organising an additional transaction within a firm with the cost of a market transaction or of organising it in another firm. He identified discovering prices and making contracts among the costs of using the market. Contracts do not disappear inside a firm, but coordination can replace a series of separate transactions. This is a theoretical framework, not a measurement of any particular company's costs.

The boundary depends on the task. When the result is easy to specify and verify, an external contract may be convenient. When work is interdependent and hard to specify in advance, internal coordination may help. The other side is that information does not automatically reach management. A job title alone does not reveal whose knowledge influences a decision.

## What happened when workers were given a voice

In a study by [Wu and Paluck](https://doi.org/10.1017/bpp.2022.9), researchers examined meetings in a manufacturing setting: 65 groups, 1,752 workers and six weeks of meetings. During the experiment, individual productivity in the intervention group increased by about 10.6% relative to the control group. Over the first 12 weeks after the experiment, 15% of workers in the intervention group and 23% in the control group left their jobs.

This is one experiment, not a promise for every organisation. The main productivity estimate covered the first six weeks from the start of the intervention, while meetings took place each week. The authors separately assessed the following six weeks after the meetings ended; there were no participatory meetings in that period. They then obtained additional data and, comparing outcomes week by week through week 12, reported a significant difference through week nine. This was a separate analysis: the result for week nine in the supplementary table depends on the variables included, and the table note does not define the “+” sign. The exact duration of the effect therefore needs careful interpretation. The study covers one country and one industry; no statistically significant difference in the variance of productivity across groups was detected, and a separate effect on average group productivity was not established in the material checked. We cannot conclude that any employee meeting will have the same result.

## When voice does not help

Consider a different situation: a team regularly fills in a suggestion form, but no one explains who reads the submissions or what happens next. The number of messages sent does not by itself show that people influence decisions. This is a hypothetical example, not a result of the experiment above.

It helps to check the whole path: is the channel accessible, can someone report a problem without fear, has a responsible person been named, is the response time known, and can a participant challenge a refusal? A separate question is whether a manager has the authority not only to receive a message but also to assign time or resources to a fix. These questions help examine how an organisation is governed; by themselves, they do not prove an improvement in outcomes.

## What remains open

The original review contains other accounts of firm boundaries, delegation and incentives. They are not equivalent to the findings of this experiment and are not used here as verified causal effects. Before publication, the study's registration and the interpretation of Wu and Paluck's weekly estimates need further checking, as do the links and the transfer of conclusions to other settings. The registration status of the extended analysis remains uncertain. This text is a draft candidate and is not ready for publication.
