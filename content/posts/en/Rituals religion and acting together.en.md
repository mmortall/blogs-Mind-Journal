People sing, pray, dance and repeat familiar movements together. Such actions can express a shared sense of belonging, but are they enough to make participants cooperate? Research offers several ways to investigate the question — and shows how hard it is to separate an effect of the ritual itself from expectations and other features of the group.

## What researchers measure in ritual

Ronald Fischer and colleagues studied nine naturally occurring types of ritual that differed in the synchrony of movements and in whether they were attributed sacred meaning. According to the article's abstract, the researchers assessed attitudes towards fellow participants and decisions in a public-goods game. The authors reported an association between synchronous movements and more prosocial attitudes, and between sacred meaning and greater contributions in the game. Their model suggests a possible mechanism, but does not by itself prove that each link in that mechanism caused the next. This account uses the available abstract; the full study was not examined. [Fischer et al., 2013](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23666518/).

The study compares particular practices and measures. Attitudes towards other participants and contributions in a game are different outcomes; neither automatically shows how a group will resolve conflicts or manage shared resources over years. Religious and non-religious practices can also differ in participants, rules and meaning, not just synchrony.

## The debate about expectations

In 2022, Atwood, Schachner and Mehr reviewed research on synchrony and prosociality and pointed to the possible role of participants' and experimenters' expectations. In their preregistered study, people assessed the expected effects of joint actions without themselves performing synchronous movements; these expectations echoed some previously published patterns, including null effects in certain conditions. The authors proposed expectations as a possible explanation for some results. This experiment shows why the concern is plausible, but does not establish that expectations explain every previously reported effect. [Atwood, Schachner & Mehr, 2022](https://mehr.nz/pdf/2022_AtwoodSchachnerMehr_OPMI.pdf).

In response, Tunçgenç and colleagues acknowledged the possibility of expectancy effects, but argued that the critique did not sufficiently account for studies with additional controls and evidence from child development. They consider that the available literature does not allow the link to be reduced entirely to expectations. This is the response authors' argument, not a definitive resolution of the debate. [Tunçgenç et al., 2023](https://www.diva-portal.org/smash/get/diva2:1823992/FULLTEXT01.pdf).

The question for readers is how the evidence was tested: did participants know what result was expected, were researchers aware of the conditions, and how comparable were the groups? Controlling for expectations helps assess an alternative explanation, but does not itself turn every collective practice into a universal tool for cooperation.

## Beliefs and choices in a game

A different question concerns beliefs about gods rather than movements. Purzycki and colleagues combined ethnographic interviews with two coin-allocation games involving 591 participants from eight communities; 35,400 observations are individual coin-allocation decisions, not people. In the games, participants allocated coins either between themselves and a distant co-religionist or between a local and a distant co-religionist. [Purzycki et al., 2016](https://www2.psych.ubc.ca/~ara/Manuscripts/Purzycki%20et%20al%202016%20Moralistic%20Gods%20Supernatural%20Punishment%20and%20the%20Expansion%20of%20Human%20Sociality.pdf).

Controlling for a range of variables, higher ratings of a moralistic god's ability to punish and knowledge of thoughts and actions were associated with more coins allocated to distant co-religionists relative to oneself and local co-religionists. This is an association between reported beliefs and choices in particular games, not experimental proof that changing belief causes cooperation. It does not measure attitudes towards all strangers or establish that religion is necessary for acting together.

## What can we conclude?

Ritual can create a shared context, and religious beliefs may be associated with how people perceive rules and other participants. These are possibilities for research, not a sufficient explanation of how a group works. Each case calls for separate study of who participates, what rules and alternative causes of behaviour exist, what is measured and to whom the results apply. The debate about expectations remains open; these studies do not imply the same result for all religions, groups and forms of cooperation.
