It is tempting to read criticism of The Venus Project as a final verdict on the idea itself. But those are different things. The failure of an organisation that promises change does not automatically prove that all of its original ideas were wrong. Conversely, an attractive idea does not exempt an organisation from showing results.

## What the source document claims

In the Google document [“The Death of Jacque Fresco’s Venus Project”](https://docs.google.com/document/d/1EI_Le4-MgLDhm1c2tpYdjjVwwpbiAk_XRsD-dKx1qH8), Yevhen Sliuzko describes a departure from the organisation’s original course, opaque management, and weak reporting on its projects. He focuses on Jacque Fresco’s lecture archive, fundraising for transcription, delays, and changes in the public programme.

These questions cannot simply be dismissed as “negativity”. Sliuzko was close to the movement and points to dates, promises, publications, and public statements. That proximity gives him context that an outsider may lack, but it also creates a conflict-of-interest risk: a former participant may see internal causes more clearly than a distant observer, while also judging the dispute through personal experience.

## Opacity is not proof of everything, but it is a serious signal

The strongest part of the criticism is not its more emotional wording. It is the question of verifiability. If an organisation raises money for an archive, announces deadlines, and promises publications, readers are entitled to a simple account: how much material existed at the start, how much was processed, what was published, how much was spent, and why the plan changed.

Inconsistent figures or postponed deadlines do not by themselves prove fraud. Archives can be incomplete, tasks can be harder than expected, and projects genuinely evolve. But when the chain “promise → action → result” cannot be followed, trust erodes even if there was no bad intent. Transparency is not decoration here. It is part of the technology of any public project.

## Where criticism must stop

The source material makes a persuasive case that there is a governance problem, but it is not an independent audit. Turning allegations into established facts would require primary records: financial reports, crowdfunding terms, archive inventories, plan versions, correspondence about deadlines, and responses from the organisation’s leadership. Without those records, it is more accurate to speak of serious questions and discrepancies than of proven corruption or an intentional destruction of the project.

There is a deeper level too. The Venus Project proposes rational resource management and automated decision-making, yet every such project still has to answer a political question: who sets the goals, who audits the algorithms, and who can stop the governing centre? If an organisation that speaks about rationality and the future operates opaquely, that is not merely a reputational problem. It demonstrates the very problem of power that its concept claims to solve.

## What remains after a critical reading

The document does not show that Fresco’s ideas are automatically right or automatically dead. It supports a more useful conclusion: attractive visions of the future should be judged by their procedures, limits, and observable results.

The criticism of The Venus Project has a strong point: it demands accountability. Its weak point is its reliance on testimony from involved participants and the absence of a complete independent audit. A reasonable position therefore lies between belief and the excitement of a takedown: check the documents, separate facts from interpretations, and do not confuse an organisation’s crisis with a final answer about the future of society.
