> This article offers a working map of state emergence: how record-keeping, resources, security, administration and feedback turn local rules into durable authority, and why a state's capacity to act is not the same as its goodness.

## Why agreeing among ourselves became insufficient

Imagine several settlements around a river. While there are few people, they can know one another, allocate water at a meeting and punish a violator through loss of reputation. But the population grows. Some live upstream, others downstream; crops ripen at different times; a grain store needs guarding; a trader arrives from far away; a land dispute can no longer be settled by elders' memories alone.

What appears is not "the state" in general, but a set of new tasks. People and property must be counted, information stored, resources collected, territory protected, decisions enforced at a distance and authority transferred after a leader dies. Those able to perform these tasks reliably gain an advantage over an order based entirely on personal acquaintance.

In this working definition, the state is not primarily a palace, flag or body of laws. It is an organization that claims enduring authority over a territory and population and creates an apparatus for observation, resource extraction, coercion, implementation, conflict resolution and recognition of its decisions as binding.

A short diagram of the article:

```text
visibility → resources → apparatus → implementation → constraints → feedback
```

The diagram prescribes no necessary sequence and is not a scale from "simple" to "advanced". These six tasks can connect in different orders and produce very different outcomes for the population.

## Four explanations to consider together

Historians and political economists have no single universal cause of state emergence. Several mechanisms compete.

### Surplus and manageable production

Settled farming can make it possible to store surplus, feed people who do not grow food, and finance specialization: officials, soldiers, scribes and engineers. Agriculture alone, however, does not guarantee a state. Conditions must permit the surplus to be appropriated, stored and protected.

### Grain, tax and records

Resources that can be counted, collected in one place and stored are especially convenient for early administration. Studies of early state formation link intensive farming, cereals, taxation and writing, while emphasizing limits on causal inference and the role of different historical paths ([comparative study of state formation](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12846917/)).

Writing in this account serves more than philosophy. It helps keep accounts, confirm obligations, plan deliveries and retain decisions when people change. But records increase both the capacity to govern and the capacity to extract resources.

### War and competition

An external threat can compel rulers to collect taxes, build roads, train a standing army and bargain with elites who otherwise would not give resources to the centre. Charles Tilly compressed this into the formula: war made states and states made war.

A modern test using European border and conflict data for 1490–1790 supports a key role for war in territorial expansion of European states at the system, state and dyadic levels. The abstract does not compare alternative mechanisms. In the introduction to the full article (Cambridge page, not the abstract), the authors contrast their result with a process driven exclusively by conquest and absorption, finding little support for that process. Mechanisms beyond this contrast, and applicability outside Europe in 1490–1790, remain uncertain ([War Did Make States](https://doi.org/10.1017/S0020818322000352)).

East Asia may limit the European comparison; the specific role of borrowing and learning was not checked in this source and needs separate study. War is a powerful accelerator, but not a universal origin of states.

### Trade, property and bargaining

A centre may be needed for more than defence against an external enemy. It can provide roads, courts, common measures, protection for transactions and rules of succession. These services do not appear automatically. Groups that pay will bargain for rights, exemptions and representation. The political capacity of a state often grows not from unilateral orders but from repeated exchange: resources for security, predictability or participation.

## The state as a set of circuits

Rather than a single strong–weak scale, consider eight circuits:

1. **Information.** The state knows who lives in its territory, what they own, where resources are and what problems arise.
2. **Extraction.** It can collect taxes, duties, labour or other resources at a cost acceptable to itself.
3. **Security and coercion.** It can defend territory and use force, but that force also needs constraints.
4. **Implementation.** A central decision reaches the road, school, port, court or municipality.
5. **Conflict resolution.** Parties know where to take a dispute and why the decision will be observed.
6. **Recognition and bargaining.** People and influential groups find the order sufficiently acceptable to cooperate rather than evade it constantly.
7. **Succession and constraints.** After a change of ruler the apparatus does not become the winner's private property; rules govern powers and transfer of authority.
8. **Feedback.** The state notices failure, permits correction and does not mistake its own statistics for reality.

Here `StateCapacity` means a set of capabilities of the apparatus, not a good state. It can be broken down into eight separate variables:

```text
StateCapacity = (I, E, C, X, J, L, S, F)
```

High `E`, the ability to collect resources, does not mean that the state judges well or provides services. High `C`, coercive capacity, does not prove legitimacy. Low `F` may hide behind attractive reports until the first major crisis.

## Why records precede many other capabilities

A modern study of the tax state is useful for its sequence. It distinguishes fiscal capacity—the ability to mobilize revenue—from informational capacity, the ability to know the population, territory and assets reliably. Using Latin American cases, the authors associate development of tax capacity with earlier creation of statistical offices, censuses and cadastral registers; in their model, information facilitates a shift from intermediary collectors to bureaucratic tax collection ([No taxation without informational foundation](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/journal-of-institutional-economics/article/no-taxation-without-informational-foundation-on-the-role-of-legibility-in-tax-state-development/5A28333CE954D59413FC38FC12B3E2C1)).

A familiar example makes the mechanism clear. Collecting income tax requires more than announcing a rate. One must know who received income and where, how to check a declaration and where to appeal an error. A census and cadastre are therefore infrastructure for action, not dull paperwork.

Visibility is ambiguous. The same database can direct aid and extract resources more precisely. A state that sees its population better gains more options; their direction depends on political constraints, incentives and the quality of feedback.

## How an apparatus outlives an individual ruler

If all decisions depend on one person, the apparatus's durability after that person's departure is in question; this is an analytical distinction, not a universal definition of a state. An apparatus becomes a state apparatus when roles are separated from persons: an office can be filled, a report checked, stores and archives remain after a leader dies, and an order operates because procedure supports it.

Bureaucracy emerges in this way. It addresses scale through standards, archives, specialization and hierarchy. There is a price: decisions slow down, local knowledge is lost, and an implementer can hide bad news behind a correct form.

Bureaucracy is therefore not the opposite of culture. It relies on norms of office, professional ethics, ideas of lawful orders and rituals of public authority. If an informal norm says "do not tell the boss about failure", a formal report will not create feedback.

## Historical sequence without a ladder of progress

A schematic evolution can be understood as layers of solutions, not a ladder from primitive to modern.

| Phase | New task of scale | New tools | New risk |
|---|---|---|---|
| Local communities | Maintain a common resource and memory of rules | Reputation, custom, assembly, local sanctions | Exclusion of outsiders; capture of rules by the powerful |
| Early cities and states | Store surplus, count people and deliveries | Writing, storage, scribes, taxes, temple or palace administration | Extraction without feedback |
| Territorial kingdoms and empires | Implement decisions at a distance | Governors, roads, archives, army, standards | Centre–periphery gap, revolt |
| Early modern state | Mobilize money and people regularly | Permanent taxes, officials, military and financial administration | Debt, fiscal overload, war as an end in itself |
| Modern administrative state | Provide mass services and regulate a complex economy | Census, statistics, schools, courts, professional services | Metrics, digital surveillance, monopoly over classification |

Older mechanisms remain in each layer. A modern ministry uses law, budgets, professional norms, national symbols and informal bargaining. Institutions change gradually: they add a new layer to an old one, alter an old rule in a new context, or turn existing infrastructure to another purpose.

### Three historical routes

The same question—how to maintain order at a distance—was answered differently. This table is not a single ladder. It offers three profiles for comparison using the same scheme.

| Route | Main task | Mechanism | Limit and risk |
|---|---|---|---|
| Late medieval Mediterranean cities | Maintain order amid trade, fragmentation and factional competition | Urban institutions, alliances, bargaining and different forms of political representation | Factional struggles and external pressure could make order fragile ([Lantschner on city states](https://doi.org/10.1093/pastj/gtab004)) |
| Early modern Europe | Regularly mobilize people and money for war and territorial administration | Permanent taxes, military and financial administration, bargaining with groups that paid | War could expand apparatus and territory; costs (extraction, debt, executive power) are not established by this source ([War Did Make States](https://doi.org/10.1017/S0020818322000352)) |
| Imperial China | Ensure succession and select officials across a vast territory | Examination bureaucracy, formal offices, service norms and local adaptation | Centralization increased continuity but created a gap between central rules and local knowledge ([Political Economy of China’s Imperial Examination System](https://www.cambridge.org/core/elements/political-economy-of-chinas-imperial-examination-system/3DA0EC88AE5901AEE641FD5686C7F8B8)) |

The comparison suggests a general principle: scale grows through a combination of records, intermediaries, resources, succession rules and ways to restrain the apparatus, not through one technology. What changes is not only the size of the state but also the cost of error for people on the periphery.

### Five specific mechanisms: where institutions come from and why they persist

To avoid confusing state emergence with later consolidation, distinguish two phases. In the first, an institution must solve a problem of scale. In the second, it must reproduce itself after a change of ruler, technology or threat. For comparison, examine five transitions:

1. **Records.** What became visible to the centre, with what delay, and through what medium—writing, seals, a cadastre, a local intermediary or oral ritual?
2. **Resources.** What was mobilized—grain, labour, money, credit or service—and who bore the cost?
3. **Intermediaries.** Did the centre replace local autonomy with direct administration or incorporate local elites into collection and implementation?
4. **Representation.** Did contributors gain channels for bargaining, audit and refusal, or did the new institution merely improve collection?
5. **Durability.** What survived the original threat and change of leader: office, archive, procedure, coalition, infrastructure or only personal loyalty?

```text
StateFormationCase = (
  phase, public_task, scale, information_carrier, legibility,
  resource, intermediary, representation, enforcement, legitimacy,
  succession, persistence, later_function_switch, alternative_explanation
)
```

Early Mesopotamian records show why writing should not become a magical first cause of the state. Seals, tokens and other material forms of accounting already connected a person with a transaction and counted goods. Writing expanded the variables that could be conveyed to a distant reader, but the first written records continued an older administrative task: accounting for resources and transactions ([Yeo, *Records, Accounting, and the Emergence of Writing in Ancient Mesopotamia*](https://doi.org/10.4324/9780429054686-3)). Comparative material from early societies adds a counterexample: non-writing systems for storing and transmitting information could operate before writing and persist afterwards. The question is not simply whether writing exists, but which medium makes the needed signal portable, verifiable and usable for action at a distance ([Bennison-Chapman, *Bookkeeping Without Writing*](https://www.nino-leiden.nl/publication/bookkeeping-without-writing)).

A quasi-experiment in colonial Mexico concerns a shift within an existing order, not the origin of the state. The patio process made silver production more visible to the Spanish crown. In mining-affected areas, the share of encomiendas transferred to direct administration, compared with non-mining areas and the period before the technology, was about 8–13 percentage points higher; this estimate comes from the [2023 working version](https://economics.yale.edu/sites/default/files/2023-03/_FiscalLegibility.pdf). The historical comparison was not randomized, and areas that never had encomiendas were excluded from the analysis. Later postal and informational investments grew more strongly there, and centralized control better survived a later crisis. This is strong evidence that legibility changes the returns to direct rule and starts a self-reinforcing loop, but not proof that this mechanism created the original state ([Garfias and Sellars, *Fiscal Legibility and State Development*](https://doi.org/10.1111/ajps.12901)).

The abstract of a study of European military history clarifies the role of resources; the full text was unavailable in this pass. After 1500 military competition made the old division between rights to collect and spend taxes increasingly untenable. The same pressure did not produce one form: some territories moved toward fiscal absolutism, others toward a parliamentary solution, while peripheral territories retained gridlock. War created demand for capacity, but institutional form depended on the initial architecture of authority—the separation of rights to collect and spend taxes. The role of intermediaries is an inference of this series and is not mentioned in the abstract ([Cox, Dincecco and Onorato, *Warfare, Fiscal Gridlock, and State Formation*](https://doi.org/10.1086/734269)).

A comparison of early modern European polities by Henriques, Karaman and Palma adds another check. Fiscal capacity and executive constraints need not grow together: high-capacity states existed with unconstrained executives and low-capacity states with constrained executives; England stands out for combining constraints with high fiscal capacity. The full article codes executive constraints for eight polities at 50-year benchmarks from 1500 to 1800, and compares them with fiscal revenue per person measured in days of unskilled wages. Thus "the state became stronger" cannot automatically be translated into "society became freer". These are distinct coordinates that must be coded separately ([Henriques, Karaman and Palma, *Fiscal Capacity and Executive Constraints in Early Modern Europe*](https://doi.org/10.1007/s11127-025-01372-4)).

Finally, formal centralization does not guarantee consent to extraction. An archival study of early Pakistan describes a case in which a centralized structure alone did not produce durable expansion of the tax base: the state failed to secure a political agreement making the tax acceptable and collectible. This is not a universal explanation of weak capacity, but a useful counterexample to the idea that adding officials and orders suffices ([Ali, *Do Institutions Matter? Taxation and State Formation in Pakistan*](https://doi.org/10.1080/02255189.2025.2454697)).

The practical lesson is to code the phase—`origin` or `consolidation`—first, then fill in the five transitions. A source showing only later tax administration does not prove state origins. Archaeological evidence of storage or ritual does not itself establish voluntariness, quality of governance or safe exit. Such analysis can, however, clarify the problem an institution solved and why the same resource yielded intermediaries, direct bureaucracy, parliamentary bargaining or persistent gridlock in different places.

## State capacity can serve different ends

In their model of the origins of state capacity, Timothy Besley and Torsten Persson connect development of legal and fiscal capacity to political incentives, stability and public goods. According to the abstract, external war as a common interest promotes accumulation of legal and fiscal capacity; the abstract does not say that the same capacity can serve a narrow group or extraction—that is an interpretation of this series, not the authors' formulation ([The Origins of State Capacity](https://www.aeaweb.org/articles?id=10.1257/aer.99.4.1218)).

One cannot infer the moral claim that a strong state is a good state. Apparatus strength and quality of ends are separate variables. A state may build roads rapidly and persecute dissenters just as rapidly. It may collect taxes without providing courts. It may have good rules on paper and be unable to implement them on the periphery.

It is useful to separate three questions:

```text
Capacity: can the state do it?
Governance: does it do so predictably and accountably?
Outcome: does this improve people's lives and freedom?
```

Confusion begins when an answer to the first question is mistaken for answers to the other two.

## State capacity can be captured

The state is not one rational actor. A minister, agency, contractor, profession and lobby can have different aims and different access to decisions. The same apparatus can therefore provide broad public benefits while entrenching concentrated benefits for a narrow group. Public choice provides an incentive check: who gains, who pays, where is the decisive gate and who can change the rule ([Buchanan, The Constitution of Economic Policy](https://www.nobelprize.org/prizes/economic-sciences/1986/buchanan/lecture/), [Oxford Handbook of Public Choice](https://academic.oup.com/edited-volume/40414)).

A diagnostic card:

```text
CaptureCase = (
  public_task, decision_gate, concentrated_beneficiary,
  diffuse_cost, agent_incentive, discretion,
  transparency, counterpower, exit, observed_outcome
)
```

These fields should not be combined into an actual index. They help distinguish capacity from good governance: check the distribution of benefits and costs, the intermediary's real discretion, independent oversight and the possibility of challenge. A group's political influence alone does not establish rent-seeking; one must show that a benefit came through changes to rules or access rather than creation of a comparable public outcome.

## Intermediaries stand between society and the apparatus

The state rarely receives requests from millions directly. Parties, unions, associations, NGOs, professional and religious organizations usually stand between people and decisions. They gather dispersed signals, aggregate demands, select negotiators and help carry an agreement along the chain. An intermediary thus lowers coordination costs: the centre need not bargain with each person separately, while members can act together more easily ([Stokes, *Political Parties and Democracy*](https://www.annualreviews.org/doi/10.1146/annurev.polisci.2.1.243), [Bolleyer, *Civil Society's Democratic Potential*](https://academic.oup.com/book/55961/chapter/439444170)).

But an intermediary does not transmit a ready-made "will of the people". It decides whom to count in its group, which demands to combine, whom to nominate and which compromise to accept. Internal participation by members must therefore be distinguished from external responsiveness to people represented by the organization. A party can be well organized and disciplined while filtering signals so that some affected people never reach the agenda.

For practical analysis use:

```text
RepresentationCase = (
  constituency, members_and_supporters, signal_access,
  aggregation_rule, selection_rule, mandate, resources,
  decision_gate, internal_voice, external_accountability,
  discipline, responsiveness, exit, observed_outcome
)
```

This card shows a second circuit of delegation. In the first, citizens give authority to a representative; in the second, an organization or party delegates to a negotiator, legislator or minister. Parties can make that chain workable and accountable, but at each successive link the risk of lost signals and an agent's independent interests increases ([Müller, *Political parties in parliamentary democracies*](https://doi.org/10.1023/A:1007073021074)).

Selection is not just a technical filter. It distributes access to offices, resources and public attention. A study of roughly 75 Western European parties and 250 leaders finds that both leaders and party organization influence candidate selection; organizational structure can constrain the leader without eliminating gatekeeping ([Marino, *Leaders or parties?*](https://doi.org/10.1177/13540688251391577)). Discipline is also ambiguous: it helps carry out an agreed programme but can make internal criticism too costly. In a Swedish survey of 1,165 politicians, misconduct was reported in about half of cases and reporting was less frequent in more disciplined parties; this is a finding from one context, not a general law ([Niklasson et al., *Misconduct and whistleblowing in political parties*](https://doi.org/10.1177/13540688241263735)).

A practical check for a state, company or closed group:

```text
signal → selection → aggregation → mandate → negotiation or execution
       → accountability → correction, replacement or exit
```

Examine who can send a signal, who discards information, who chooses the representative, how the mandate is recorded, what happens to disagreement and whether the intermediary can be replaced without losing basic rights. Without this check, growth in administrative capacity can be mistaken for growth in representation.

## An institution can begin with one function and end with another

It is easy to imagine a later parliament as a ready-made constraint on the monarch. History is usually more complicated. An assembly might be summoned from above to gather nobles, secure order or coordinate a campaign. It could then become a place where the ruler obtained taxes, credit or troops. Only under certain conditions did participants acquire a durable right to demand accounts, take part in rule-making or withhold resources.

Distinguish three events:

```text
appearance of assembly → change of function → consolidation of powers
```

Evidence from the Crown of Aragon in 1100–1327 shows that early summons were more often associated with establishing order and succession. After the transition to a representative type, taxes became more important; as powers expanded, war became more important. A competing model for early medieval Europe emphasizes that assemblies could initially be the ruler's own coordination device rather than a consent procedure; over time the same institution could become a forum for oversight ([Møller, *The Birth of Representative Institutions*](https://doi.org/10.1017/ssh.2017.6), [Young, *Consent or Coordination?*](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.irle.2022.106096)).

In a study of 16 European polities in 1000–1600, changes of monarch brought temporary concessions, but durable institutional change required mutual interest between ruler and elites. A study of German towns in 1290–1710 associated conflicts with larger and more elective councils, guild participation and more complex local taxation. The authors use whether the best-connected local noble’s first child was a daughter or son as an instrument for conflict. The instrumental-variable analysis includes only grid-cell decades in which the most-connected noble had a recorded firstborn. A causal interpretation depends on this instrument affecting the outcomes through conflict rather than other channels; the authors discuss and test a possible alternative channel. The result is not a law for all societies ([Kokkonen and Møller, *Succession, power-sharing and the development of representative institutions*](https://doi.org/10.1111/1475-6765.12381), [Becker et al., *Wars, Taxation, and Representation*](https://doi.org/10.1093/jeea/jvaf019)).

A European parliament was not the only way to connect the centre and local groups. A comparison of China and Europe describes Chinese guilds, native-place associations and local elites that at different times financed services and infrastructure and took part in consultation, but had a different status and balance with official administration. Thus absence of a European parliament does not mean absence of intermediaries; compare autonomy, appointment, resources, access to decisions and ability to challenge the centre ([Mokyr, *Social organizations and political institutions: why China and Europe diverged*](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/ecca.12508)).

Late Qing reform gives a more specific contrast. In 1898–1911 officials and elites established local councils; the exact number is not confirmed by the materials available here. Information about local changes was gathered in provincial centres and Beijing, and reform formed part of an attempt to turn subjects into citizens. Representative form could both broaden participation and increase central visibility; the book description does not prove the councils were effective or autonomous ([Thompson, *China's Local Councils in the Age of Constitutional Reform*](https://www.jstor.org/stable/j.ctt1tg5md9)).

A practical comparison card:

```text
RepresentationEvolutionCase = (
  initial_initiator, initial_function, participants,
  resource_or_problem, selection_rule, representation_scope,
  right_to_convene, right_to_withhold_or_audit,
  enforcement, function_switch, persistence,
  downstream_capacity, excluded_groups, alternative_explanation
)
```

The key question is not whether this was a parliament, but who convened it, what problem it addressed, who controlled its agenda and when participants acquired their own right to demand accountability. Otherwise temporary concession is mistaken for an institution and coordination for consent.

## Why states persist and why they break down

An institution persists when its procedures continually reproduce resources, roles, expectations and support. A census justifies a statistical office's budget; the budget sustains the census; tax finances the apparatus; the apparatus makes tax more collectible. This is positive feedback.

The loop can become vicious. Poor information produces an ineffective tax; tax intensifies pressure on visible groups; pressure reduces cooperation; lack of cooperation worsens data. A regime may respond to failure with further centralization, reducing signal quality.

Crisis opens a window for change but does not determine the outcome. War, epidemic, financial collapse or technological change can strengthen executive power, create services, destroy old bargains or transfer powers to a private intermediary. The result depends on existing coalitions, norms, knowledge and constraints.

### Succession is a distinct technology of governance

A change of ruler tests whether a system has become institutionalized. If archives, office, appointment procedures and budget sources survive the person, capacity can persist. If everything rests on a personal entourage, the leader's death turns the apparatus into an object of struggle.

```text
SuccessionPersistenceCase = (
  leader_change, succession_rule, uncertainty, elite_bargaining,
  bureaucratic_memory, resource_continuity, representation,
  conflict, capacity_after_change, practice_retention,
  excluded_groups, alternative_explanation
)
```

In a 2017 working paper, Kokkonen and Sundell report descriptive country-year frequencies of civil-war onset: under primogeniture, 5.1% in years with a monarch's natural death versus 2.3% in years without one; under other succession systems, 17.7% versus 3.2%. These comparisons do not establish that succession rules alone caused the differences. The authors discuss selection and alternative explanations. The working paper is a separate version; its full text has not been compared with the final 2020 article ([2017 working paper, Table 4](https://www.gu.se/sites/default/files/2020-05/2017_9_Kokkonen_Sundell.pdf)). Hereditary rule is not thereby shown to be fair, accountable or effective.

Daniel Steven Smith examines over 300 Eurasian dynasties from 1000 to 1799. His main dataset uses dynasty-century observations to study relationships between Inner Asian cavalry warfare, father-to-son succession and conquest; supplementary analyses examine reign length. In a supplementary instrumental-variable analysis, he uses border distance from Inner Asia as an instrument for the Inner Asian cavalry warfare (IACW) score and regards the result as support for a causal interpretation of the association with father-to-son succession. He argues that warfare could favor militarily capable rulers while limiting the potential for long, secure reigns and cumulative state capacity. The principal models do not directly measure accumulated administrative capacity, so that part is an interpretation rather than a demonstrated outcome. Historical coding and alternative geographic explanations also limit a simple causal reading ([Smith, *Storm from the Steppes*](https://doi.org/10.1017/S000305542510097X)).

In a 2018 author manuscript, Acharya and Lee study the availability of male heirs to medieval European rulers under succession norms that privileged male relatives. Their indirect comparison relates several heir measures for 1000–1500 to later regional development; it does not directly observe every succession dispute or separately establish each link in the proposed chain from conflict through weaker state institutions. Their analysis excludes some region-years, including those under urban republics or Muslim rulers, so it is not a test of all European societies. The authors interpret the associations as evidence of path dependence: contingent births mattered in interaction with gender-biased norms ([author manuscript, sections 2 and 4](https://web.stanford.edu/~avidit/europe.pdf), [2019 article abstract](https://doi.org/10.1177/0010414019830716)).

Power transitions are not always destructive. In a working paper, Jonathan Doucette studies 293 cities in the European north–south corridor from the Netherlands to Switzerland in 1000–1400. He finds that cities were more likely to gain self-government in the year after a ruler's natural death; he examines how walls and lordship characteristics condition the relationship. His historical heir-supply measure is based on sons of previous rulers, not simply the number of the deceased ruler's current heirs. The transition measure relies on the first recorded council or assembly, and evidence on who could participate is scarce. The study relies on assumptions about death timing and historical coding; some politically motivated deaths may be misclassified as natural. The idea that succession can also redistribute powers between centre and intermediaries is this series author's interpretation ([Doucette, working paper](https://becklab.hms.harvard.edu/sites/projects.iq.harvard.edu/files/pegroup/files/doucette.pdf)).

A practical test after leadership change should measure the system, not just the new leader: decision delays, preservation of budgets and archives, changes in intermediaries, conflict counts, access to appeal and changes in peripheral outcomes. If quality of action disappears with the person, that is evidence of personal dependence. If procedure survives but voice disappears, capacity outlived the leader but accountability did not.

A useful change profile:

```text
StateChange = (
  external shock,
  internal coalition,
  resource,
  information technology,
  new circuit of authority,
  old rule,
  reproduction mechanism,
  power constraint,
  outcome,
  losers,
  alternative explanation
)
```

It prevents saying "the state emerged because of war" without checking who invested in the apparatus, how it collected information, which rights were exchanged for resources and why the new order survived the particular conflict.

### State capacity must reach the person

Taxes, numbers of officials and uniform orders show that an apparatus can collect and transmit resources. They do not answer the practical question: who received water, health care, a road, protection or a fair hearing, and at what cost? Use a separate card:

```text
EverydayOutcomeCase = (
  task, recipient_group, input_resource, chain_of_authority,
  local_knowledge, service_accessibility, quality, distribution,
  tax_burden, coercive_burden, voice/appeal,
  outcome_persistence, alternative_explanation
)
```

A study of Chinese counties associates stronger local fiscal capacity with economic output and public goods, including education and health care. In an earlier author working paper, Cheng and Gawande use estimated population losses during the Taiping, Nian and Dungan rebellions of 1851–1880 as an instrument for contemporary local government revenue per capita. They argue that this variation supports a causal interpretation and present checks, but that interpretation depends on assumptions about how historical losses affect present outcomes; it is not established by the instrument alone. The full 2022 article with Qi has not been compared with this earlier version, so its exact specification remains unchecked here. These results do not establish that a stronger state improves every outcome ([earlier working paper](https://www.isid.ac.in/~epu/acegd2019/papers/KishoreGawande.pdf), [2022 article](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.worlddev.2022.105990)).

In post-Soviet Russia, decentralization did not produce the same outcome everywhere. Roberto Foa compares 83 federal subjects using a state-history index that weights earlier periods by the presence, local basis and territorial reach of government, with greater weight for recent periods. His spatial regressions associate higher scores with some later outcomes, including improved road surfacing and less inadequate housing; associations with health and public transport are weaker. A comparison of Tatarstan and Buryatia suggests that local elites and their ties to citizens might be one mechanism. This observational comparison does not establish that historical statehood alone caused the outcomes or that delegation works only under one set of conditions ([Foa, *Decentralization, Historical State Capacity and Public Goods Provision in Post-Soviet Russia*](https://www.roberto.foa.name/Foa_World_Development.pdf)).

Another counterexample cautions against equating a service with its purpose. Gasparyan compares villages in one Indian region on opposite sides of former boundaries between direct and indirect colonial rule. Her primary indicators are the presence of paved roads and health facilities in the 2011 census, not measured colonial investment. In supplementary analysis of high-school availability (Appendix J), the author describes the Mysore–Bombay result as no effect and the Hyderabad–Bombay result as a statistically significant positive effect. Table J.1 shows dependence on bandwidth, however: for Mysore the 10 km estimate is positive and significant at 5%, while estimates at 20, 50 and 100 km are not significant at that level; for Hyderabad the positive estimates at 10–50 km are marked significant at 10%, and the 100 km estimate at 1%. This measures school presence, not educational quality. Historical extraction incentives are part of the explanatory model, not a directly established motive for each construction. Boundaries were not random, so causal interpretation depends on assumptions of the spatial comparison ([Gasparyan, *Indirect Rule and Public Goods Provision*](https://www.cambridge.org/core/services/aop-cambridge-core/content/view/14B22C39335E465E4612A265CCD8063B/S2049847023000316a.pdf/indirect-rule-and-public-goods-provision-evidence-from-colonial-india.pdf)).

In practice, check not only the volume collected but five transitions:

```text
resource → authority → local implementer → access and quality → opportunity to complain
```

If only the first increases, this is extraction capacity. If all five increase, there is a basis for discussing capability that serves people. Even then, check whether excluded groups bear extra burdens for the visible average result.

## What to check in practice

To assess a state institution, ministry or municipal reform, start with the path of action rather than a law's title:

1. What problem is the apparatus meant to solve?
2. How does it learn that the problem exists?
3. Who decides and who implements locally?
4. What resource is needed for implementation and where does it come from?
5. Who can hide bad news or appropriate resources?
6. Where can a person turn when an error occurs?
7. Can the leader, procedure or provider be changed?
8. What happens if a pilot produces no result?

Check answers using four types of data: records and budgets, actual implementer behaviour, people's local experience, and comparison with a similar place or period. One official report shows intention; it does not prove actual implementation.

## What this means for the future of governance

Digital systems give states cheaper ways to count, classify and forecast. They can speed services while raising the cost of error and expanding surveillance. Digitalization therefore sharpens rather than removes old questions:

- who defines a person's category;
- whether an incorrect record can be corrected;
- whether an independent appeal channel exists;
- who sees primary data;
- how reuse of information is constrained;
- whether an automated decision can be switched off without losing a basic service.

A good future state is not one that knows everything about everyone. It obtains sufficiently accurate information for a specific task, explains decisions, constrains its own power and corrects classification without destroying a person's life.

## Conclusion

Local norms and personal ties may be insufficient for some problems of scale, but this is neither the sole nor a universal cause of state emergence. The state brings records, resource extraction, security, implementation, courts, recognition of order, succession and feedback into one apparatus.

History provides no single cause. Agriculture may create opportunities, grain and records may facilitate accounting for resources, war may facilitate mobilization, trade may increase demand for predictability, and bargaining may link resources to rights. These are possible mechanisms, not a mandatory chain; each has a cost.

The most useful formula is not to ask whether a state is strong, but to break its capacity into circuits and separately check whom it serves, who constrains it and whether a person can correct an error or leave a bad procedure.

## Counterexample: a strong apparatus does not mean a good state

An administration can quickly count people, collect tax and implement an order, while directing that capacity toward a narrow group, suppression of criticism or extraction without public benefit. High state capacity shows that an apparatus can act; it does not answer for whom it acts or who constrains it. Assessing a state must therefore combine capacity, outcome quality, equality of rules, appeal and reversibility of decisions.
