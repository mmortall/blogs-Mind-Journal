To act together, people need to understand what to expect from one another. Some expectations are set out in an organisation's rules; others exist in habits and ideas about what is acceptable. Let us call culture an infrastructure for coordination as a working metaphor: it helps shape such expectations, but it does not by itself guarantee trust or a good outcome.

## What to examine

```text
expectation → evaluation of an action → possible response from others → repetition or change of the rule
```

This is a map of questions, not a proven causal chain. What do people expect? Do they consider a departure from the rule wrong? Can others respond? Does the practice change, and who can propose a change? Habitual behaviour and approved behaviour do not necessarily coincide.

## When similarity is associated with approval of helping

Carla Handley and Sarah Mathew studied normative beliefs in nine clans across four pastoral ethnic groups in Kenya: Turkana, Samburu, Rendille and Borana. A questionnaire on cultural beliefs was completed by 759 people; all but four also evaluated 16 described scenarios. Another 34 Rendille respondents answered only scenarios with Samburu as the other group.

Participants judged whether the character in a story acted rightly by helping a stranger or refraining from harming them. In the analysis, greater cultural similarity between groups was associated with more frequent endorsement of such actions across group boundaries, accounting for distance. These are responses to described situations, not observations of actual help. The study does not establish that similarity caused cooperation. The authors consider cultural group selection as an explanatory hypothesis; the data themselves do not prove this mechanism or make the result universal. [Handley and Mathew, 2020](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41467-020-14416-8); [open copy of the paper](https://dyrehaugen.github.io/recsi/pdf/Handley_2020_Cooperation.pdf).

## When pressure preserves a harmful rule

A shared expectation can also sustain behaviour that harms participants as a group. In a laboratory experiment by Klaus Abbink and colleagues, contributions were higher with the opportunity for mutual punishment than without it under both payoff rules. Under one rule contributions reduced the group's total payoff; under the other they produced no group gain before punishment costs. A second experiment examined the first rule, and most participants judged refusal to contribute to be socially inappropriate. This shows that such an outcome is possible under the conditions of a particular game, not that all punishment is harmful or that every stable norm is inefficient. [Abbink and colleagues, 2017](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41467-017-00731-0); [PubMed abstract](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28931829/).

## How to distinguish coordination from submission

When evaluating a rule, it matters to ask whom it helps and who bears the costs. Similar behaviour may arise from shared belief, material interest, a formal requirement or fear of consequences. Observing coordinated actions alone does not allow us to choose one explanation.

A practical assessment includes the right to report a problem, a clear procedure for consideration and appeal, and the ability to correct the rule and exit without disproportionate punishment. These are questions about an organisation's design, not conclusions established by the two studies above. If disagreement cannot be expressed safely, outward unity does not by itself show that participants agree.
