This is a working world map of direct critics of The Venus Project, adjacent political thinkers, civic organisations, and open governance tools. It does not claim that all participants agree or that every relationship has been independently audited.

Only public original materials are included. Confidential reports, private correspondence, and unverified accounts are deliberately left out.

The filters preserve three distinctions that are easy to lose. A direct critic comments on The Venus Project itself; an adjacent author addresses a similar problem without belonging to that dispute. A deliberation platform helps a group understand opinions; it is not automatically a secure election system. Finally, open source makes code inspectable, but does not make the institution democratic, representative, or safe.

Each entry therefore records not only an idea, but also what it proposes instead, its present status, and a serious caveat. Inclusion is not endorsement.

The diagram uses two axes tailored to this debate rather than the usual party labels. Horizontally, ideas move from public or resource planning through hybrid coordination to markets and private autonomy. Vertically, they move from expert or technocratic control to participation and direct democracy. “Left”, “centre”, and “right” therefore mean positions inside this specific design problem, not a complete verdict on a person’s politics. A point locates the governing mechanism represented by the entry; reasonable readers may place it differently.

These are editorial hypotheses reviewed on 31 August 2026, not measurements. A person, organisation, document, or tool may be useful as a reference while the plotted point refers only to a documented mechanism.

Click a point to open its explanation. The first link always returns to that entry in this Mind Journal article; the links after it lead to original sites, papers, archives, or source code.

## Glossary for reading the map

These labels are analytical shortcuts, not fixed identities. A project can combine several of them or move between them in practice.

### Political orientation

- **[Political spectrum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Political_spectrum)** — a model that places political positions along one or more axes. This map uses its own two axes and should not be mistaken for a universal political test.
- **[Left-wing politics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Left-wing_politics)** — a broad family that usually gives greater weight to social equality, public provision, labour, or common ownership. “Left” does not by itself mean central planning.
- **[Centrism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Centrism)** — positions combining elements associated with left and right or favouring incremental compromise. A centrist is not necessarily politically neutral or exactly halfway on every question.
- **[Right-wing politics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Right-wing_politics)** — a broad family often giving greater weight to private property, markets, tradition, hierarchy, or national continuity. These commitments do not always appear together.

### Who should make decisions?

- **[Technocracy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Technocracy)** — government in which technical experts receive substantial decision-making authority because of specialised knowledge. A technocrat is an expert exercising policy power, not simply someone who uses technology.
- **[Meritocracy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Meritocracy)** — allocation of roles or rewards by demonstrated ability and achievement. Its central dispute is who defines merit and whether unequal starting conditions are ignored.
- **[Participatory democracy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Participatory_democracy)** — institutions that involve citizens more continuously than periodic elections.
- **[Direct democracy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Direct_democracy)** — citizens decide policies directly instead of leaving every decision to elected representatives.
- **[Deliberative democracy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Deliberative_democracy)** — legitimacy is sought through informed public reasoning, exchange of arguments, and revision of views, not voting alone.
- **[Liquid democracy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Liquid_democracy)** — participants may vote directly or delegate their vote, often by topic, while retaining the ability to withdraw that delegation.
- **[E-democracy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/E-democracy)** — use of digital systems for participation, deliberation, voting, or accountability. Digital delivery does not automatically make a process democratic.

### How should economy and society be organised?

- **[Economic planning](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Economic_planning)** — deliberate coordination of production and allocation through plans rather than relying only on market prices. Planning can be centralised, decentralised, democratic, or algorithmic.
- **[Post-scarcity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Post-scarcity)** — a hypothetical condition in which advanced production makes many important goods abundant. It does not mean that every resource becomes infinite or conflict disappears.
- **[Resource-based economy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Venus_Project)** — The Venus Project’s proposal to organise access and production around measured resources and technical capacity rather than money. It is a movement-specific programme, not a settled academic model.
- **[Libertarian municipalism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Libertarian_municipalism)** — Murray Bookchin’s proposal for democratic local assemblies linked in confederations. Here “libertarian” refers to anti-authoritarian communal politics, not simply free-market libertarianism.
- **[Social ecology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Social_ecology_(Bookchin))** — a tradition connecting ecological crises with hierarchy and domination in society.
