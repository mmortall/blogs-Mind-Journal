A kinship group, a government agency, a firm and a digital platform can carry out different tasks: passing on information, coordinating action, distributing resources and resolving disputes. Comparing these tasks is useful if we do not turn the comparison into a story of an inevitable transition from one form to another.

The existence of a register, an office or an algorithm says something about how work is organised, but does not by itself show whether decisions are fair or whether a person can challenge them. New tools can coexist with personal relationships and informal rules.

## What to compare

For a particular case, we can ask several questions: who makes a decision, what information is available to them, who checks for errors, who pays for the procedure, and can a participant exit? This is a working framework for comparison, not a measured regularity of historical development.

For example, recording an obligation can help pass information to someone who was not present when an agreement was made. But the record can also contain an error or disclose unnecessary data. To assess a particular system, we need to examine both its benefits and the possibility of correction.

## What the historical sample shows

In a Seshat study published in *PNAS* in 2018, Peter Turchin and colleagues described information on 414 polities occupying 30 selected geographic areas at different times. They coded 51 underlying variables, combined into nine social complexity characteristics, including population, territory, hierarchy, governance and information systems. [Turchin and colleagues, published text](https://discovery.ucl.ac.uk/id/eprint/10041342/1/PNAS-2018-Turchin-E144-51.pdf). A [2020 reanalysis](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41467-020-16035-9) describes the dataset as identical but reports 414 data points for 285 unique polities. In the checked 2018 supplementary files, the 414 records have distinct combinations of geographic area (NGA), supplied polity identifier (PolID) and Time, but contain 285 distinct PolID strings. The [2018 supplementary methods](https://www.ebi.ac.uk/europepmc/webservices/rest/PMC5777031/supplementaryFiles) describe treating polities spanning centuries as separate entries and a 285-entry resample. The checked 2018 data file is byte-identical to the 2020 authors’ pinned analysis file; this does not establish the identity of other versions or independently verify that each identifier denotes a distinct historical polity. The PCA and imputation were not independently audited.

After handling missing data using multiple imputation, the authors applied principal component analysis to the nine characteristics. The first component described roughly three-quarters of their observed variation in this analysis. This is not a share of human history explained, nor a percentage of well-being, fairness or quality of governance. Historical information is incomplete, and imputed values depend on the procedure used; these records should not be treated as independent random samples of the whole world.

The component shows how the selected characteristics vary together, not a single obligatory path of development or a cause of transitions. A high value on a composite measure does not prove that people were better off or that a particular institution was accountable.

## What a model of change proposes

In another Seshat-based study, published in *Science Advances* in 2022, researchers compared many combinations of 17 potential predictors for three measures of social complexity. Among the terms highlighted in the best-supported models were measures of agricultural productivity and military technology, particularly the combined spread of iron metallurgy and cavalry. [Turchin and colleagues, full text](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9232109/).

The authors interpret the results as evidence of a causal role for these factors, using dynamic models and historical time-series data. They also caution that the method is insufficient for definitive causal claims: omitted variables and other data limitations are possible. This was not a randomised experiment and does not prove that every war or population increase inevitably creates a state. Different historical trajectories cannot be reduced to a single sequence of institutions.

## From comparing history to questions about a platform

The studies above did not examine modern digital platforms as a continuation of the historical sequence they measured. Their result cannot be directly transferred to platforms. However, the same comparative framework can be used to ask questions: who sets access rules, how is an incorrect record corrected, who can see the data, and what does a person lose by leaving?

These questions do not prove that a platform resembles a state in every important respect. They help describe specific powers and their limits. Assessment requires separate evidence about rules, practice and consequences for participants.

Larger scale or more complex infrastructure can expand opportunities for coordination, but they are not measures of fairness in themselves. Historical indicators answer some questions; assessing rights, the distribution of costs and the ability to appeal requires other observations.

> Editorial limitation: this article draws on the two cited Seshat studies and does not verify every historical claim in the original material about kinship, cities, empires and platforms. The discussion of modern platforms here consists of questions for separate research, not an empirical finding of those studies. No author or language review has been performed.
